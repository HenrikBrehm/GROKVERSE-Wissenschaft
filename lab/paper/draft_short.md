## Abstract (Entwurf, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

Networks that grok modular arithmetic use a few Fourier frequencies, whose choice is usually attributed to initialization. We show that the training set's composition steers this choice. In pre-registered replications on fresh seeds, engineered splits enrich five prescribed frequencies in the embedding of a one-layer transformer for addition mod 113 and 97, multiplication mod 113 and the dihedral group D59 (50 seeds per task), and equal-norm embedding perturbations along the steered frequencies reduce the logit margin more than along controls (20 seeds per task). In addition mod 113, even a uniform random 30 % split weakly biases the learned frequencies. Engineered splits generalize 6.5× sooner than uniform random splits and 7.5× sooner than a control matched in pairs per output (addition mod 113), and 4.5–7.4× sooner than both in addition mod 97 and multiplication mod 113 (geometric means, steps bounded at 8000; 20/20 units each). The choice starts to persist within 25–50 full-batch steps (addition mod 113, learning rate 10⁻³): the sign of an ablation contrast between old and new target frequencies reverses between data switches after 25 and 50 steps (it tracks embedding energy), and a switch after 25 steps delays grokking by 1.8×, whereas one to new pairs with the same targets did not, on average. These results are full-batch; under i.i.d. minibatches (batch 256, 64) the steering was confirmed, and at learning rate 10⁻³ the onset of persistence grew roughly as the square root of the batches per epoch (batch 1024–64, confirmed, energy measure); a fixed data order did not shift the frequencies at 30 % data (pilot). In one 14 M-parameter language model trained from scratch (≈ 79 % arithmetic tokens; 10 fresh seeds), the chosen facts also steered the tied number embeddings, and ablating the steered frequencies cut median held-out accuracy from 0.98 to 0.05 (other split's targets: 0.98).

## 1 Introduction (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43, Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung; Quelle: Langfassung §1, §1b und [[paper/related_work]])

Small networks trained on modular arithmetic first memorize their training pairs and only much later generalize ("grokking"; Power et al. 2022). The generalizing solution computes with a handful of Fourier frequencies of the group (Nanda et al. 2023), or with a few irreducible representations for other finite groups (Chughtai et al. 2023). *Which* frequencies a particular network uses has been described as arbitrary across runs (Chughtai et al. 2023); many frequency sets implement the task equally well (Gromov 2023), and at a fixed training set, hyperparameters and initialization can even change the type of algorithm (Zhong et al. 2023). Recent theory attributes the competition between frequencies to the initialization (He et al. 2026a). For uniform data it derives the order of learned representations from the Fourier statistics of the input encoding (Marchetti et al. 2026). With one-hot tokens that encoding favours no frequency in cyclic groups and ties irreducible representations of equal dimension, and the role of the train–test split has been named as open (He et al. 2026b).

We ask whether the training set itself can decide this choice. In the one-layer transformer of a previous grokking study, with fixed one-hot tokens and full-batch AdamW, we construct training splits whose pair mask concentrates its spectrum on five prescribed frequencies. This lever differs from three known ones. LeDoux (2026) showed that a fixed, structured order of examples sets the fundamental frequency under minibatch training, with the same frequency across random training subsets. Marchetti et al. (2026) work through encoding statistics. In recursive feature machines, training sets that are invariant under a subgroup of the task's symmetry group prevent generalization, and the learned feature matrix then encodes only that subgroup (Tomàs Bernal et al. 2026; kernels only, symmetric splits, no prescribed frequencies). Here only *which* pairs are shown changes, the split has no built-in symmetry, and the targets are prescribed frequencies. Salah and Yevick (2024) restricted the training set of modular addition by removing symmetry-related pairs, but measured only whether the network generalized, not which frequencies it learned. In language models and related architectures, data, architecture, optimizer and tokenizer decide whether periodic number features become geometrically separable (Fu et al. 2026). For word embeddings, co-occurrence symmetry explains circular and other low-dimensional structures in theory, matched empirically in language models (Karkada et al. 2026). Our setting isolates the data lever in a task where the learned algorithm is known. A first-order argument accounts for the initial enrichment: the Fourier power of the first embedding gradient follows the coherent spectrum of the training-pair mask (post hoc, Appendix G). It does not predict what we measure beyond that: the enrichment survives grokking, comes with a functional sensitivity, speeds up generalization, and becomes persistent within tens of steps.

We report three main pre-registered results, and a minibatch extension (Section 6), with fresh seeds and frozen analysis manifests. (1) Engineered splits steer the learned frequencies in four tasks, including a non-abelian group, and networks are more sensitive along the steered frequencies; an ordinary random split also biases the choice, though far from determining it (Section 3; sensitivity follows spectral energy). (2) Engineered splits generalize several times faster than uniform random splits and than a control with the same number of pairs per output (Section 4). (3) The choice starts to persist within 25 to 50 full-batch steps (at learning rate 10⁻³). A switch after 25 steps delays grokking by about ×1.8, one after 50 steps does not, and a switch to new pairs with the same target frequencies costs no time on average (Section 5). That early training can resist later data is familiar from critical learning periods (Achille et al. 2019), the primacy bias (Nikishin et al. 2022) and warm starting (Ash and Adams 2020). In fine-tuning, fixed-clock optimizer memory makes data-order effects first order in the learning rate (Sweeney 2026). What we add, not found in our search, is a measured time scale of tens of steps for a prescribed feature choice under a controlled data switch.

Because LeDoux's order effect arises under minibatch training, Section 6 (L0) tests whether the steering survives minibatch training and a structured example order that favours other frequencies. Apart from one small language-model configuration (LM1, Section 7), all claims hold for one model family and for constructed splits; R4 and Section 5 hold for addition mod 113 only, except the persistence window in embedding energy (also addition mod 97 and multiplication mod 113, R11); the speed-up of Section 4 (R5) was also confirmed for these two tasks (R12). Explorations, resets, the optimizer clock of the lock-in, and a first-order account are in the appendix. Novelty statements mean "not found in our search", not "new".

## 2 Setup (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43, Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung; Quelle: Langfassung §2)

**Model and training.** One-layer transformer of the original GROKVERSE study (following Nanda et al. 2023, without biases): embedding W_E and positional embedding (d_model = 128), one attention layer with four heads of width 32, a ReLU MLP of width 512, no layer normalization, untied readout. Inputs are (a, b, =); the loss is the cross-entropy at the last position. Training is full-batch AdamW (learning rate 10⁻³, weight decay 1, β = (0.9, 0.98)) on 30 % of all pairs. The two arms of a seed share the initialization; only the training set differs. Confirmation blocks ran on one GPU after a statistical equivalence check against the CPU path; runs compared within a block used the same device.

**Tasks.** Addition mod 113 (primary) and 97, multiplication mod 113 (frequencies in the discrete-logarithm coordinate, Z_112; pairs with a zero operand are not in the group and keep uniform weight in the split construction), and composition in the dihedral group D59 (order 118; the 29 two-dimensional irreducible representations).

**Engineered splits.** Per seed, two disjoint target sets of five frequencies each. Arm F draws 30 % of all pairs without replacement with weights q(a, b) = max(0.05, 1 + ε·w(a, b)), w(a, b) = (1/5) Σ_{k∈T_F} cos(2πk·u(a)/n + φ_k(c)) with the group coordinate u(a), the output c and a random phase per target and output (for D59 an irrep trace); arm G likewise; ε = 2. Token counts are no more unequal than in a random split (coefficient of variation 0.14 in the pilot construction), although their spectrum is also target-enriched (Appendix G). The main difference lies in the spectrum of the pair mask (centred target share 0.194 against 0.089 for a random split).

**Switch protocol (Result 3; addition mod 113).** A common held-out test set (20 %) is removed first; two pair-disjoint training sets A and B of 3831 pairs pick their pairs in alternation from their own weighted orders (steering strength calibrated to a mask target share of 0.185). A run trains t steps on A, switches to B (optimizer state kept) and trains a fixed further budget; each seed runs both directions.

**Measures.** On the group-element rows X of W_E, P_k(X) is the power in the Fourier (irrep) subspace of k. *Steering*: Δ_pow = log[P_F(T_F)/P_F(T_G)] + log[P_G(T_G)/P_G(T_F)] at step 2000. *Margin sensitivity*: the drop of the mean correct-class logit margin on pairs outside both splits after a step X − δ·u_S, δ = 0.02‖X‖, along the arm's own target set S (u_S = P_S X/‖P_S X‖, the normalized projection of X onto the frequency (irrep) subspaces of S), minus the mean drop along four control sets (drawn without both target sets and the harmonics 2k), averaged over both arms. *Persistence*: π = (L_switch − L_pureB)/(L_pureA − L_pureB), L = log[P(T_A)/P(T_B)] of the final embedding, averaged over both switch directions (an unbounded affine index, not a probability). *Ablation contrast*: accuracy after removing the new target subspaces from W_E minus accuracy after removing the old ones. *Time to generalization*: first evaluation with test accuracy ≥ 0.95, evaluated every 25 steps and bounded at a fixed horizon: in R5 a stable event (≥ 0.95 at s, s + 25 and s + 50 after train accuracy ≥ 0.99), bounded at 8000 steps; in R6/R8 the first crossing counted from step 0, bounded at 2000, compared as log2 ratios with the destination-only run.

**Protocol.** Every confirmation block was registered before its runs: hypotheses, primary measure, minimum effect, family with Holm correction, sample size, a reserved block of fresh seeds, and the previously seen data with the decisions they influenced. A committed manifest stores code hashes, and the analysis accepts only runs bound to it (Appendix H); the frozen version of a registration is the one at its manifest commit (some file names still carry the suffix DRAFT from drafting). Holm correction applies within each block's family; no correction is applied across the blocks of Table 1. Intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over seeds. Pilots are reported separately as explorations. Blocks are named by short identifiers (P1, R4, …); Table 1 maps each identifier to its hypothesis, manifest commit, fresh seeds and number of runs (generated from the frozen manifests, `figures/table_registrations.py`).

## 3 Result 1: the composition of the training set steers the learned frequencies (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**Steering (P1, pre-registered replication on 50 fresh seeds per task; tasks chosen as steerable in pilots).** For each seed we built two engineered splits F and G of the same size whose pair masks concentrate their spectrum on five disjoint target frequencies (irreducible representations for D59), and trained the same initialization on each. Only the split differs, so the comparison is a paired intervention. At step 2000 the relative W_E energy of the own against the other targets, Δ_pow, was 8.24 [7.92, 8.54] for addition mod 113, 8.09 [7.75, 8.43] for multiplication mod 113, 6.11 [5.79, 6.43] for addition mod 97 and 2.64 [2.55, 2.73] for D59, positive in 50/50 seeds in every task (Holm-p ≤ 1.5·10⁻⁵, worst case and at the resolution of 200 000 random sign flips; Figure 1). Descriptively, the five own targets carried on average 0.80 of the embedding's group power in addition mod 113 (chance 0.089), 0.79 in multiplication mod 113, 0.69 in addition mod 97 and 0.44 in D59 (chance 0.17). For D59, step 2000 is before grokking; after 12 000 steps (P2f; 17/20 seeds generalized) the own irreducible representations carried a median 0.98 of the energy. In the other three tasks the steering is not a pre-grokking transient: in separate stored runs (P2d/P2e, 20 other seeds per task; post hoc exploration, not P1) Δ_pow stayed positive in every seed at every stored step and grew after generalization (numbers in Appendix M). The steering also held when one hyperparameter deviated (R1; weakest where few runs had generalized by step 2000), in a shared-embedding MLP (at 10 000 steps, after grokking), at 4× width, in a two-layer transformer (smaller: −2.70 [−3.16, −2.18] against one layer; R7), and for non-modular integer addition, where no network generalized, so there it is a property of the representation only (P4). Details in Appendix A.

**Functional sensitivity (P2d, P2e, P2f).** A step of norm 0.02·‖X‖ (X = number rows of W_E) along the steered frequencies reduced the mean correct-class logit margin on pairs outside both splits more than steps along four pre-specified control sets: 0.250 [0.229, 0.270] (addition mod 113, 4000 steps), 0.291 [0.268, 0.308] (multiplication mod 113, 8000 steps), 0.294 [0.275, 0.312] (addition mod 97, 8000 steps), each 20/20 seeds, and D = 0.233 [0.214, 0.253] for D59 after 12 000 steps (positive in 20/20 seeds; 17/20 had generalized). In the addition-mod-113 switch models where we tested it (not the P2d–P2f models), this sensitivity followed the energy distribution: at equal removed energy the dominant set had no advantage. We therefore claim sensitivity, not use beyond energy. Three earlier registered functional variants were not evaluable, aborted or not confirmed; they are reported in Appendix B.

**An ordinary random split also shapes the choice (R4, addition mod 113, 60 fresh units).** Each unit crossed two initializations with two uniform 30 % splits (8000 steps). Networks sharing a split had more similar embedding spectra than networks sharing nothing: after 8000 steps, Δ_split = 0.118 [0.079, 0.157] in an energy-weighted cosine (48/60 units positive, 42/60 above the minimum effect), and before grokking the rank order of all 56 frequencies was also more alike (Spearman at step 2000, 0.33 [0.30, 0.36]). Descriptively the shared split raised similarity more than a shared initialization, but it is far from determining the choice: networks with the same split share on average about one of their five strongest frequencies, against about one half without a shared split (chance 25/56 ≈ 0.45). This concerns representation energy, not use (no ablation), in one task and one architecture. The engineered splits of Result 1 are thus consistent with a stronger dose of an effect that already acts on ordinary random splits.

**What this shows and what it does not.** In a paired design with engineered splits, the choice of training pairs enriches the prescribed frequencies in four tasks, with fixed one-hot tokens and full-batch training. Section 6 (L0) tests whether the steering survives minibatch training and a competing order signal. LeDoux (2026) reports the same frequency across random training subsets under a structured example order at p = 9973, plausibly a different regime (not measured) with ≈ 80× more pairs, in which the spectral fluctuations of a uniform random subset, shrinking roughly as 1/√n, are about 9× smaller.

## 4 Result 2: engineered splits generalize earlier (R5; Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**Design (pre-registered, 20 fresh units, addition mod 113, 8000 steps).** Per unit, seven training sets of 3831 pairs share one held-out test set and one initialization: a uniform random split R, engineered splits with ε = 2 for two target sets and ε = 1 and 8 for one of them, and for each ε = 2 split a control D with exactly the same number of training pairs per output value (per anti-diagonal), but with positions drawn uniformly within each anti-diagonal. The event is a stable rise of test accuracy to ≥ 0.95 after train accuracy ≥ 0.99; times are bounded at 8000 steps.

**Result.** The engineered split generalized in ×6.5 [5.3, 7.9] fewer steps than the random split (target set A) and ×7.5 [6.5, 8.6] fewer than the matched control (mean over both target sets; geometric means of bounded time ratios; 20/20 units in the same direction for both; Holm-p ≤ 2·10⁻⁵, at the resolution of 100 000 permutations; Figure 2). Higher ε was faster (ε = 1 took ×14.4 [12.4, 16.6] as long as ε = 8; secondary, outside the family; ε changes pair selection and truncation together, so this is not by itself evidence for concentration). Median bounded times were 775–813 steps (ε = 2), 6163 (R) and 6850–7513 (D). By step 8000, 16/20 random-split units and 14/20 and 11/20 control units had generalized; the others count at the bound, so the ratios are conservative.

**What this shows and what it does not.** At matched per-output counts, the arrangement of the pairs within the anti-diagonals accounts for the speed-up relative to D (this arrangement carries both the concentrated mask spectrum and a target-enriched spectrum of the token counts, which D does not separate); the output marginals alone do not produce it. D matches only the frozen statistics, so finer geometric differences are not excluded. "Earlier" means an earlier stable rise within 8000 steps, not a higher final accuracy. Known levers on the grokking delay include the initialization scale and weight norm (Kumar et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023; Khanh et al. 2026), and that early task-aligned embedding structure shortens it is known from other interventions (Howe 2026b; GrokTransfer); and that the makeup rather than the size of the training data governs how fast grokking proceeds was reported for implicit reasoning, where speed tracks the ratio of inferred to atomic facts (Wang et al. 2024; there, changing the ratio also changes the training size). The contribution here is that the choice of training pairs alone shortens the delay, at matched size, initialization and test set. Limits: one task in this block (see R12 below for two more), one architecture and optimizer setting (lr 10⁻³, weight decay 1), train fraction 30 %, ε = 2 for the primary contrasts.

**Other tasks (R12).** In a pre-registered replication on addition mod 97 and multiplication mod 113 (R12, 20 fresh units per task, the same design transferred to each task's group coordinates, i.e. the discrete logarithm for multiplication, 8000 steps), engineered splits again generalized earlier than the random split (target set A; addition mod 97: ×4.50 [3.76, 5.40]; multiplication mod 113: ×7.42 [5.92, 9.06]) and than the per-output-matched control (mean over both target sets; ×4.60 [4.02, 5.24] and ×7.14 [6.00, 8.36]; geometric means of bounded time ratios); 20/20 units were ordered as predicted in each of the four tests (Holm over four tests, p_Holm ≤ 4·10⁻⁵ at the resolution of 100 000 sign flips). Many control runs had not generalized by step 8000 (random split: 6 and 8 of 20; matched controls for target sets A/B: 11/6 and 5/9 of 20), whereas all engineered runs had; the control times are bounded at 8000 as for R5, so the ratios are conservative (per-unit times: supplementary figure `figures/fig16_r12_speed.py`). Independently recomputed.

## 5 Result 3: the choice starts to persist within 25–50 full-batch steps (P3, N28, N29, R6, R8, R11; mainly addition mod 113; Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**Persistence after a switch (P3, N28; pre-registered, 30 fresh seeds each).** After 25 steps on a first engineered split and 2000 steps on a pair-disjoint second split, final models sat at π = 0.351 of the normalized log-energy distance between the two pure endpoints (persistence π); the excess over the best tested static additive trace was 0.293 [0.263, 0.322] (30/30), where a trace is the π of the pure-destination embedding plus c times the embedding change of the first 25 steps, maximized over c ∈ [0, 1]. Zeroing Adam's first moment at the switch lowered π (separate block N28, 0.340 → 0.189), and ten extra pre-switch steps compensated this, compatible with a reading in which longer exposure to the source split builds the persistence (Appendix M); that the first moment carries such a signal is known from other settings (Sweeney 2026; Xu 2026).

**An ablation contrast reverses (N29, 20 fresh seeds).** Removing all five target frequencies from W_E after a switch and 4000 further steps: after a switch at step 50, removing the old targets cost 0.903 [0.846, 0.953] more test accuracy than removing the new ones (20/20 seeds above 0.3); after a switch at step 25, removing the new targets cost 0.603 [0.498, 0.700] more (17/20). The embedding energy points the same way (post hoc), so the contrast does not separate reliance from energy. The two time points bracket a reversal; a network-wide or irreversible commitment is not shown.

**A switch inside this window costs time (R6, 30 fresh seeds).** With b the first step with test accuracy ≥ 0.95 (bounded at 2000) and C(t) the log2 time ratio against training on the destination split only, a switch at step 25 delayed grokking by C(25) = +0.85 [0.73, 0.96] (a factor ≈ 1.8; 29/30 seeds; Figure 3) and more than a switch at step 50 (C(25) − C(50) = +1.25 [0.97, 1.50]; 28/30). Across the grid (t = 10, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 75), the mean cost was positive at t = 10–40 (at 40 only +0.27, 22/30) and negative at 50 and 75 (switched runs then generalized earlier on average, 24/30 seeds at 50 and 30/30 at 75, so C(t) is a net effect of interference and of progress made on the engineered source split). C(t) compares regimes: t destination updates are replaced by source updates, no mixed A∪B control was run, and time to threshold is evaluated every 25 steps. Switch runs that had not generalized by step 2000 count at the bound (all destination-only runs had), so C(t) underestimates the delay where it is largest (counts in Appendix M). The confirmed difference between 25 and 50 argues against a reading in which the cost only reflects fewer updates on the destination split, which predicts C(50) ≥ C(25).

**A change of targets costs far more than a change of pairs (R8, 25 fresh seeds).** A switch at step 25 to a pair-disjoint split with the same five targets and matched steering strength did not delay grokking on average (−0.15 log2; 8/25 seeds slower), whereas the switch to other targets did (+0.66; 24/25); difference +0.81 [0.57, 1.04]. The delay is therefore not a generic cost of an early change of training pairs, nor of fewer destination updates alone. Because the same-target split has partly reversed wave phases, a pure phase conflict is not excluded. Several switch runs had not generalized by step 2000 (A→B 20/25, B→A 15/25 did; same-target switches 24/25 each), so C_AB is bounded. One task, one architecture.

**The window generalizes beyond addition mod 113 (R11, the same 30 fresh seeds for both tasks).** With a one-direction switch at 25 or 50 steps, the first split's choice persisted mostly after 50 but not after 25 steps also in addition mod 97 (π(25) = 0.38, π(50) = 0.90; difference +0.52 [0.45, 0.59], 30/30 seeds) and in multiplication mod 113 (0.27 and 0.74; +0.47 [0.40, 0.54], 30/30); all six registered tests held (Holm). This is the embedding-energy measure only; the ablation contrast and the time cost were not re-tested there (details in Appendix M).

## 6 L0: set composition against data order under minibatch training (Kurzfassung; gekürzt in Sitzung 48, der bisherige Volltext mit allen Zahlen steht unverändert in Anhang J unten)

**Question.** All results above use full-batch training. LeDoux (2026) found, under minibatch training with a fixed, structured example order, the same fundamental frequency across random training subsets. We ask (i) whether the steering by the set survives i.i.d. minibatch training, (ii) what happens when a fixed order favours other frequencies than the set, and (iii) when the choice starts to persist under minibatches.

**Design.** Addition mod 113, the setup of Section 2, AdamW with i.i.d. minibatches of 256 or 64 pairs (a pilot with 8 seeds, also ran full batch with a matched 30 000-step budget and SGD with momentum). The conflict arm uses a fixed stride order after LeDoux (pairs sorted by (a mod 10, a), identical in every epoch), whose spectral weight lies on O = {11, 12, 22, 23, 34, 45}; the engineered targets exclude O. Because a first-order computation made before any run (Appendix J) predicted that the order can compete with the set within the full-batch lock-in window only for long epochs, an arm with batch 16 (240 steps per epoch; weight decay 0.1, because weight decay 1 did not generalize there in 30 000 steps, a regime change) was added before any run. LeDoux's order effect was strong at 0.3 % data (≈ 1165 steps per epoch); our regime is far from it. Readings were fixed before the runs; the conflict is judged only if the order acts at all.

**Steering survives minibatch training (confirmed).** On 10 fresh seeds (L0, frozen before any run), Δ_pow was 9.43 [9.06, 9.81] at batch 256 and 9.60 [8.79, 10.46] at batch 64 (10/10 seeds each; Holm-p 4·10⁻¹² and 3·10⁻⁹; Figure 4a). In the pilot, the same batch sizes gave 9.60 and 9.85 against 10.28 for full batch with the same budget. At batch 64, 12 of 20 confirmation runs had not settled at the end of the budget (final train accuracy below 0.99), so the claim is steering at the end of the budget, not in converged networks. The steering also held at batch 16 with weight decay 0.1 (pilot, 8 seeds: 9.21 [8.82, 9.60]; Appendix J).

**Order against set (pilots, exploration).** At batch 256 and 64 the stride order did not shift the learned frequencies (peak frequency in O in 0/16 runs each), so by the pre-set rule the conflict was not testable there, nor at batch 16 (order-alone reading 'unclear'). Descriptively, at batch 16 (weight decay 0.1; pilot EXP-106, 8 seeds), the order raised the order share on random splits (+0.16 [0.07, 0.24]) but not on the engineered splits, where it only delayed generalization (≈ ×1.6, 15/16 pairs). This is consistent with LeDoux's remark that the importance of order scales inversely with the content signal of the data. SGD with momentum could not be calibrated and is not evaluable.

**The lock-in window under minibatches (N54b confirmed; full-batch comparison from pilots).** In pilots, a switch after 25 or 50 steps at batch 256 left no persistent trace (8 seeds; π ≈ 0, against π(50) = 0.87 in full batch on the same seeds), and the window lay later in steps but earlier in epochs (pilots: half-point ≈ 133 steps = 8.9 epochs at batch 256, against ≈ 31 steps = 31 epochs in full batch; energy measure). On 10 fresh seeds (N54b, frozen before any run, 840 runs) the half-point t½ at lr 10⁻³ was 58, 114 and 289 steps at batch 1024, 256 and 64; it grew with the steps per epoch N/B (Figure 4b, c) with a mean log–log slope α = 0.583 [0.560, 0.606] (registered square-root band [0.3, 0.7]; Holm-p 5·10⁻⁶). Doubling the learning rate raised the slope in every seed (Δα = +0.139 [0.098, 0.178], 10/10, Holm-p 6·10⁻⁵): it shortened lock-in to 0.58 [0.54, 0.62] of its duration at batch 1024 but only to 0.85 [0.78, 0.92] at batch 64. log t½ is curved (two-point slopes 0.48 and 0.68 at lr 10⁻³), so α is an average slope over N/B = 3.7–60, not a clean power law; with N fixed, steps per epoch and batch size are not separated. In our search (29 September 2026) we did not find a prior measurement of a square-root batch-size scaling of an early lock-in time at fixed learning rate. The pilots, a model comparison fixed before the N54b results and all limits are in Appendix J.

## 7 Discussion and limitations (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**What the three results say together.** In small networks trained full-batch on these tasks, which pairs are shown steers which frequencies the embedding favours, strongly for engineered splits and weakly for random ones (Result 1; measured as embedding energy). In addition mod 113 and one architecture, engineered splits also generalize several times faster at fixed size (Result 2). In the same setting, full-batch and at the default learning rate and weight decay, the choice starts to persist within 25 to 50 steps, as measured by embedding energy and by an ablation contrast that does not separate reliance from energy (Result 3). None of this requires changing the tokens, the architecture or the loss.

**Beyond the toy model (LM1 and LM1-F, confirmed on the same 10 fresh seeds; LM3 and LM3-F on 10 further fresh seeds; details in Appendix K).** We also trained a 13.9 M-parameter transformer from scratch on TinyStories text with interleaved arithmetic facts (addition mod 113, about 79 % of the tokens; loss on the text and on the answer tokens; tied input and output embeddings; 6000 steps). The engineered fact splits steered the number-token embedding (Δ_pow 5.32 [4.86, 5.73], 10/10 seeds above the registered minimum of 1; held-out fact accuracy 0.90–0.995 in all 20 runs; these splits also carry the targets in the number counts, so this does not isolate a pair-structure effect), and removing the steered frequencies impaired the model: removing the run's own five target frequencies from the number-token rows lowered median held-out accuracy from 0.98 to 0.05, whereas removing the other split's targets left it at 0.98 (more than 0.05 lower in 2 of 20 runs; mean difference 0.88 [0.84, 0.92], 10/10 seeds). Both effects held again at a lower arithmetic share (LM3, about 8.7 % arithmetic tokens: Δ_pow 5.99 [4.64, 6.99], 9/10 seeds above the minimum of 1; ablation contrast 0.84 [0.75, 0.91], 10/10 seeds with a positive contrast; Appendix K). LM1 and LM3 share one architecture, one task and tied embeddings, so the ablation cannot tell whether the input or the output side uses the frequencies.

**Limitations.** (i) *Regime:* unless stated otherwise, results use full-batch AdamW (lr 10⁻³, weight decay 1, β = 0.9/0.98) and a train fraction of 30 %. R1 varies one hyperparameter at a time for the steering. The reversal of Result 3 held at double learning rate (switch at 12 against 50 steps) but its switching side was not confirmed at 0.3× weight decay (Appendix A, R2). Minibatch training and data order are tested only in L0 and N54b (Section 6); the 25–50-step figure of Result 3 is a full-batch result. An exploratory attempt to test the steering without Adam, with full-batch SGD with momentum 0.9 (learning rates 0.05 and 0.1, four seeds, EXP-126), was not evaluable: at 0.05 none of 8 runs fitted the training set within 20 000 steps, and at 0.1 all 8 diverged; since the L0 SGD arm (Appendix J) was also not evaluable, all results are AdamW results. (ii) *Scope:* steering holds in four tasks; other architectures (MLP, 4× width, two layers) and functional sensitivity were each tested in fewer settings (Appendices A, B and E). R4 and Result 3 hold for addition mod 113 in one architecture only, except that the persistence window of Result 3 (energy measure) was also confirmed for addition mod 97 and multiplication mod 113 (R11); the speed-up of Result 2 (R5) was also confirmed for these two tasks (R12), in the same architecture. Steering also held in one small language-model architecture at about 79 % (LM1; ablation LM1-F; above) and about 8.7 % arithmetic tokens (LM3; ablation LM3-F; Appendix K). (iii) *Measures:* functional sensitivity follows spectral energy in the addition-mod-113 switch models where this was tested (not in the P2d–P2f models). We make no claim about use beyond energy; this includes the LM1-F and LM3-F ablations, whose own and other target sets also differ in energy. Resets (Appendix C) localize sensitivity, not mechanism. (iv) *Time scales* are counted in optimizer steps. Which clock governs the persistence is only partly settled: it is not the step count alone (R3, descriptive; its clock of summed learning rate, Λ = Σ lr, was not confirmed), and it matches summed learning rate in one confirmed equivalence cell (lr × 0.25) and is consistent with it in a second (lr × 2: direction confirmed, equality descriptive) once Adam's moment windows are rescaled (R9, addition mod 113, π measure; Appendix D). Under minibatches the learning-rate effect depended on the batch size (N54b), so no single summed-learning-rate clock holds there. (v) *Horizon:* switch experiments are read at most ≈ 5000 steps after the switch (full batch 2000–4000), other final models at 2000–12 000 steps (minibatch L0: 30 000); much longer training is untested, and representation metrics measured at grokking need not be converged; for example, effective rank kept compressing for tens of thousands of steps after generalization (Khanh 2026b). (vi) *Design history:* many decisions were informed by pilots on seeds that were already used, and each registration lists them. Every confirmation used fresh reserved seeds and a frozen analysis manifest or code gate. Post hoc statements (e.g. energy direction, scalar Adam estimate, equal-energy test) are not confirmations.

## Anhang J (Kurzfassung): L0 und die Minibatch-Uhr der Festlegung im Detail (bisheriger Text von §6, in Sitzung 48 unverändert hierher verschoben)

**Question.** All results above use full-batch training. LeDoux (2026) found, under minibatch training with a fixed, structured example order, the same fundamental frequency across random training subsets. We ask whether the steering by the set survives minibatch training, and what happens when an order favours a different frequency than the set.

**Design (pilot EXP-100; the confirmation follows as its own registration).** Addition mod 113, the setup of Section 2. Per seed, engineered splits F and G whose five targets exclude the frequencies favoured by the order, and a uniform random split R. Arms: full batch for 2000 and for 30 000 steps (a budget-matched reference); i.i.d. minibatches of 256 and of 64 pairs (AdamW); SGD with momentum 0.9 (batch 256; learning rate calibrated beforehand on separate runs); and a fixed stride order after LeDoux, pairs sorted by (a mod ⌊√p⌋, a) = (a mod 10, a), identical in every epoch (batch 256). The stride order puts its spectral weight on frequency 11 (49 %), then 23, 34, 12, 45 and 22 (O); the sets carry no more weight on O than chance. The random split with i.i.d. and with stride order measures the order effect alone. A feasibility step before the pilot tests whether the stride order acts at all at 30 % data, at batch 256, 64 and 16; if it acts only at a smaller batch, the conflict arm is also run there (with i.i.d. and stride arms at that batch size), and if it acts at neither, the conflict is reported as not testable at 30 % data, as fixed before the runs. Rationale, computed before any run from the split files (first order: no optimizer, no weight decay; at most a necessary condition): under full-batch training the frequency choice starts to persist within the first 25–50 steps (Result 3), and summed over whole epochs the stride batches reproduce the set. On the random split (mean over 8 seeds), the excess order-frequency share of the summed batch masks over i.i.d. batches is 0.005 after 50 steps at batch 256 (15 steps per epoch); at batch 64 (60 steps per epoch) it is 0.147 after 25 steps, 0.047 after 50 steps and 0 after one full epoch. The value at 50 steps lies at the edge of our criterion, half of the engineered split's target excess (0.5 × 0.094 = 0.047; 2 of 8 seeds fall below it), so whether the order can compete within the lock-in window at batch 64 is open and is decided empirically. LeDoux's order effect was strong at ≈ 1165 steps per epoch (only the stride order generalized) and modest at about 10 steps per epoch (p = 97, 26.5 % data; fixed orders ≈ 20 % faster); our regime at batch 256 lies on the modest side. At batch 16 (240 steps per epoch) the same first-order computation gives an order excess of 0.194 after 50 steps, twice the set's target excess, and only 0.21 epochs fit into the lock-in window, so even i.i.d. batches carry less of the set's spectrum early on (early target share 0.10 against 0.18 at batch 256); batch 16 was added to the feasibility step for this reason, before any run.

**Readings (fixed before the runs).** Steering under minibatch: Δ_pow (as in Section 3) with confidence intervals per arm, also at equal step count (2000). Conflict: "set wins", "order wins" or "both act", judged only if the order is shown to act (otherwise "not testable"); measured by Δ_pow under the stride order, the order frequencies' share of the embedding energy, and the peak frequency. Speed: time to generalization in steps and epochs, engineered against random split.

**Result (pilot EXP-100, 8 seeds, exploration; Sitzung 46; all numbers independently recomputed).** With i.i.d. minibatches of 256 and of 64 pairs, the engineered splits still steer the learned frequencies (Δ_pow 9.60 [8.98, 10.21] and 9.85 [9.00, 10.73] at the end of 30 000 steps, against 10.28 [9.24, 11.21] for full batch with the same budget); the steering of Result 1 is not a full-batch artefact in this setting. As predicted by the first-order dilution estimate (which assumed the full-batch window; see below), it is weaker at batch 64 at equal step count (at step 2000: full batch 8.36, batch 256 8.05, batch 64 7.29); at the end of the budget this difference is gone. At 30 % data a fixed stride order did not shift the learned frequencies at either batch size (peak frequency in O in 0/16 runs at each; order-share difference on the engineered splits +0.004 [−0.003, 0.015] and +0.005 [−0.000, 0.011]), as a first-order estimate predicted for batch 256 (hypothesis: an epoch of 15 steps is shorter than the lock-in window); for batch 64 it predicted an edge case, and the feasibility runs showed an order effect there only through one seed (+0.05), the pilot none; the order effect reported by LeDoux (2026) at 0.3 % data (an epoch of ≈ 1165 steps) does not appear in this regime. On a random split the evidence for an order effect is intermediate (peak in O in 0/8 and 1/8 stride runs, but an order-share difference whose CI lies below 0, driven by i.i.d. runs (4 of 8 seeds at batch 256, order share up to 0.57) whose random split itself favours O); we report it as unclear. SGD with momentum could not be calibrated (no learning rate generalized in both calibration seeds) and is not evaluable. Engineered splits generalized ≈ 4.3× faster than random splits also under minibatches at batch 256 (median 77 against 333 epochs; pilot only, descriptive, not part of the confirmation). **Limits:** pilot seeds; at batch 64 many runs were not settled at the end (final train accuracy below 0.99 in 7/16 i.i.d. and 14/16 stride runs), so its end values are read partly from unstable networks. At batch 16 (240 steps per epoch) the network did not generalize with the default weight decay 1 within 30 000 steps (3 feasibility seeds, random split; test accuracy ≤ 0.73); with weight decay 0.1 it learns, and the order-against-set test in this long-epoch regime (EXP-106) and the confirmation on fresh seeds are reported below. **The lock-in window under minibatch (pilot, 8 seeds, switch at 25 or 50 steps, both directions).** The full-batch reference replicated on these seeds (π(25) = 0.38, π(50) = 0.87; difference +0.49 [0.39, 0.59]). Under i.i.d. minibatches of 256, the early lock-in of Result 3 did not appear by 50 steps: the choice does not yet persist after 50 steps (π(25) = −0.01, π(50) = −0.02; difference −0.00 [−0.05, 0.05]). Post hoc, at an equal horizon (step 2000) π was also ≈ 0, so this is not an effect of the longer training after the switch. At batch 256, 50 steps are 3.3 epochs, against 50 epochs in full batch; a follow-up (EXP-107, same 8 pilot seeds, switch at 100–1500 steps, exploration) found the window later in steps and earlier in epochs: π(100) = 0.33 [0.24, 0.42], π(200) = 0.85 [0.78, 0.92], π ≈ 1.00 from 375 steps (25 epochs) on; the half-point lies near 133 steps (8.9 epochs) against about 31 steps (31 epochs) in full batch (linear interpolation; π read at step 5000 here and at step 2000 in the reference; energy measure; one batch size; independently recomputed). So a lock-in (energy measure) also appears under minibatches of 256, but it follows neither a pure step nor a pure epoch clock. A batch series on the same 8 seeds, with predictions fixed before the runs (EXP-108; exploration; independently recomputed; its mean half-point at batch 256 is 127 steps, averaged per seed, against ≈ 133 read off the mean π above), placed the half-point at about 62 steps for batch 1024 (predicted 60–67 by a square-root clock anchored at full batch or at batch 256) and 290 for batch 64 (predicted 240–266; later than predicted): the number of steps to lock-in grew roughly as (N/B)^α with α = 0.54 [0.52, 0.56] (0.556 [0.513, 0.595] without the full-batch point), i.e. with the square root of the steps per epoch. One simple reading, derived in this project (AI-assisted, see the AI use disclosure) from the sign-like updates of Adam (Li et al., 2024, whose sign model gives an expected update ∝ √B·μ/σ while √B·μ/σ is small; cf. the Adam SDE of Malladi et al., 2022), is that lock-in occurs once the data-driven drift has covered a fixed distance; it predicts t½ ∝ 1/(lr·√B). At double learning rate (EXP-109, 2 of the seeds, pilot, not independently recomputed) the half-point halved at batch 1024 (≈ 31 steps) but shrank only by ≈ ×0.75 at batch 64, so this reading is at best partial. **Confirmation (N54b, frozen before any run, 10 fresh seeds, batch 1024/256/64 at learning rates 10⁻³ and 2·10⁻³, 840 runs; independently recomputed).** At lr 10⁻³ the mean slope of log t½ against log(N/B) was α = 0.583 [0.560, 0.606] (registered square-root band [0.3, 0.7]; Holm-p 5·10⁻⁶), with half-points of 58, 114 and 289 steps. Doubling the learning rate raised the slope in every seed (Δα = +0.139 [0.098, 0.178], 10/10, Holm-p 6·10⁻⁵): it shortened lock-in to 0.58 [0.54, 0.62] of its duration at batch 1024 but only to 0.85 [0.78, 0.92] at batch 64. So the square-root clock replicates on fresh seeds at the default learning rate, and the simplest sign-drift model (t½ ∝ 1/(lr·√B)) does not suffice, mainly at small batches (Δα between batch 256 and 64: +0.21 [0.13, 0.29]; between 1024 and 256: +0.07 [−0.01, 0.14]); log t½ is curved (two-point slopes 0.48 and 0.68 at lr 10⁻³), so α is an average slope over N/B = 3.7–60, not a clean power law; with N fixed, steps per epoch and batch size are not separated. In a model comparison fixed before the N54b results (exploration), t½ ≈ 23.6·√(N/B)/λ + 2.2·(N/B) (λ = lr/10⁻³; a drift term plus about two epochs) fitted the six cell means better than a free power law. In our search (29 September 2026) we did not find a prior measurement of a square-root batch-size scaling of an early lock-in time at fixed learning rate.

**Confirmation (L0, frozen before any run, 10 fresh seeds; Sitzung 46, independently recomputed).** The steering under i.i.d. minibatches was confirmed at batch 256 (Δ_pow = 9.43 [9.06, 9.81], 10/10 seeds, Holm-p 4·10⁻¹²) and at batch 64 (9.60 [8.79, 10.46], 10/10, Holm-p 3·10⁻⁹). At batch 64, 12 of 20 runs had not settled at the end of the budget (final train accuracy below 0.99; descriptive, post hoc as announced; H2 therefore claims steering at the end of the budget, not in converged networks). Data order and the lock-in window were not part of this confirmation (the lock-in window under minibatches is confirmed separately in N54b, above). **Long epochs (EXP-106, pilot, 8 seeds, batch 16, weight decay 0.1).** Steering held (Δ_pow = 9.21 [8.82, 9.60]); by the pre-set rule the conflict was not testable (no shift on the engineered splits, order-share difference [−0.005, 0.012]; order-alone reading 'unclear', peak in O in 3/8 random-split runs). Descriptively, on random splits the order raised the order share by +0.16 [0.07, 0.24] (+0.14 without the three seeds seen in the probe; about 0 in the three seeds whose i.i.d. run already carried 0.20–0.24 on O), and on the engineered splits it delayed generalization by 0.67 log2 [0.42, 0.93] (≈ ×1.6, 15/16 pairs; no delay on random splits). In this pilot the order thus shifted the spectrum on random splits but not on engineered splits, where it only cost time; this is consistent with LeDoux's remark that the importance of order scales inversely with the content signal of the data.


## Anhang K (Kurzfassung, Entwurf Sitzung 48): Towards language models (exploration, not part of the confirmed claims)

*All results in this appendix except the two LM1 confirmations (LM1 and LM1-F, fresh seeds 4000–4009, marked as such) are explorations, with readings fixed before each run unless marked post hoc or descriptive; they are not confirmations. Seeds are given in each experiment record. Results marked 'independently recomputed', and the 60 000-step result of the next-token-loss paragraph, were recomputed with independent code; several other numbers (e.g. the Format paragraph, EXP-121 and EXP-123) were checked against the stored runs by an independent reviewer (see the experiment records); the others were not.*

**Question.** Does steering by the training set (Result 1) survive when the task is trained the way a language model would see it? We changed one property at a time in a trainer that is bit-identical to the Section 2 setup when all switches are off (EXP-102; checked on 3 seeds, split F, at snapshots 50, 250, 1000 and 2000), with the engineered splits F and G of 10 seeds and 2000 full-batch steps.

**Format.** An explicit operator token (a + b =), a softmax over the whole vocabulary, and tied input/output embeddings each kept the steering clearly positive (pre-set reading 'holds'; paired differences to the reference between −0.76 and −0.05, CIs including 0): Δ_pow 7.41 [6.74, 8.17], 7.99 [7.52, 8.48] and 7.28 [6.38, 8.21] against 8.04 [7.59, 8.51] for the reference, 10/10 seeds each. With all three switches together and i.i.d. minibatches of 256 (EXP-112, 5000 steps), Δ_pow was 9.96 [9.44, 10.47] against 9.86 [9.43, 10.28] for the reference at the same batch size (paired +0.10 [−0.20, 0.40]).

**Next-token loss.** A language model also predicts the operands. With the loss on every position, the network must predict b after a, which it can only memorize for the training pairs. This delayed grokking by more than an order of magnitude (median 750 steps with the loss on all positions except b, 20/20 runs by 2000; 9 000–46 000 steps with it, 9/10 seeds by 60 000). After grokking Δ_pow was 4.40 [3.83, 4.91], below the reference in 9/9 seeds (paired −3.75 [−4.68, −2.97]), i.e. about 55 % of its value at step 2000; the pre-set reading was 'unclear' because the interval contains half the reference value. The comparison is at different step counts (22 000–47 000 against 2000). Training the reference longer did not close the gap in this exploration: its Δ_pow rose from 8.04 at step 2000 to 10.44 at 10 000 and 10.54 at 30 000, and at equal step counts the loss on all positions stayed below it in 10/10 seeds (−7.36 [−8.22, −6.31] at 10 000, −6.26 [−7.20, −5.15] at 30 000; exploration, independently recomputed). Equal step counts are not equal stages, however: with the loss on all positions only 1/20 runs had generalized by 10 000 and 13/20 by 30 000; in the 4 seeds in which both runs had generalized by 30 000 the gap was still negative in each (−2.6 to −6.8). Without the loss on the b position the steering held in full batch (8.26 [7.55, 8.96]), but under minibatches of 256 in the full language-model format it was smaller and grokking slower at 5000 steps (8.24 [6.78, 9.72]; paired −1.62 [−3.16, −0.13]; 13/20 runs generalized). At 10 000 steps 16/20 runs had generalized and the paired gap was −1.15 [−2.40, 0.08] (8/10 seeds negative), which by the pre-set rule reads as 'only delayed' (independently recomputed): no lasting weakening was shown, which is not the same as equality (the count lies exactly at the rule's threshold of 16/20, and the upper CI bound only just exceeds 0: 0.08, 0.06–0.14 across bootstrap seeds). Informally, a similar pattern (full-sequence loss favouring a memorized table over the algorithm) was reported for a composition task in the dihedral group D3 in a blog post (read via a summary tool only); we did not find a measurement of this delay or of its effect on data steering. In contrast, Lin, Zhang and Xu (2025) found next-token training on the whole sequence to generalize better than training on the answer tokens only on benchmark reasoning datasets during pretraining (answer-only training was more effective for finetuning; abstract read); our setting differs in that the operands come from a fixed fact table and can only be memorized.

**Token frequencies.** In a language model, token counts could shape the number embeddings without any arithmetic. In the Section 2 model (answer loss, full batch), splits with exactly equal counts of every a, b and c = a + b (every number 34 times in every role; EXP-104, 2 seeds) still steered (Δ_pow > 1 in both seeds at all three levels of symmetry breaking, 6.0–10.1; not comparable in size with Result 1, because their mask target share is 0.64–0.73 with 0 or 10 swap moves and 0.27–0.30 after 500, against 0.17–0.19), so there the steering does not need a frequency signal. Nearly shift-invariant versions of these splits generalized in only 2 of 8 runs by 8000 steps, against 4/4 after stronger symmetry breaking, in line with the observation of Tomàs Bernal et al. (2026) for kernels that symmetric training sets can prevent generalization (2 seeds, descriptive; at the two lower levels 6 of 8 runs never generalized, so their Δ_pow describes non-generalized networks).

**A small language model (explorations).** A 13.9 M-parameter transformer trained from scratch on TinyStories with interleaved arithmetic sentences (tied embeddings; loss on the text and, inside the arithmetic sentences, on the answer tokens only, except where noted) learned the facts in all three configurations with this answer-only loss (below); with 8.7 % arithmetic tokens and the loss on every position (EXP-105, 2 seeds, 10 000 steps) one seed began to learn them and the other did not, so arithmetic share and loss mode are confounded there (EXP-116 below separates them); with about 79 % arithmetic tokens (EXP-114, 1 seed, P1-type splits) both runs of the F/G pair learned them between steps 1750 and 2000 and reached 0.98 and 0.99 accuracy on held-out facts by step 4000, and Δ_pow on the number-token rows was 5.60. With about 34 % arithmetic tokens and the same answer-only loss (EXP-113, answer arm, 2 seeds, 16 000 steps), the models learned the facts late (seed 3862 began to fit them only at step 9000, after about 2.3 million fact presentations, against 1.2 million at 79 %; seed 3863 at step 6250), reached 0.95–0.98 held-out accuracy, and the fact selection steered the number embedding (Δ_pow 7.00 and 4.79 at step 16 000; pre-set reading 'holds'). With only about 8.7 % arithmetic tokens and the answer-only loss (EXP-116, 2 seeds, 16 000 steps), the facts were again learned (held-out accuracy 0.70–0.95; 0.5 first reached between steps 6250 and 12 250) and steered (Δ_pow 2.06 and 4.97; 2.06 and 4.72 at step 15 000, before the text stream wrapped once; pre-set reading 'holds'), so in these explorations the steering did not need a high arithmetic share. Descriptively (post hoc), Δ_pow peaked shortly after the fit onset (250–500 steps later) and then fell in one of the two EXP-116 seeds (4.32 at step 6250 to 2.06) and in both seeds of the EXP-113 all arm below; in 5 of these 6 runs the energy of the run's own targets also fell in absolute terms, whereas in the equal-count splits below it still grew in all four runs, but the other arm's targets gained share in 5 of 6 as well (reading fixed before computing: own targets fade in 4 of 6 runs, other arm's targets grow in 2). With the loss on every position at 34 % (EXP-113, all arm), the training facts were memorized (0.94–0.96) but held-out accuracy stayed at 0.11–0.51 by step 16 000 (pre-set reading 'unclear'); Δ_pow was still above 1 (2.06 and 1.76, after 3.48 and 3.80 at the fit onset), and post hoc (reading fixed before computing) two of the four models showed a partial commutativity pattern (0.28 and 0.56 on held-out facts with a trained mirror against 0.03 and 0.05 on the others; reading 'partial'). At 34 % (same seeds, splits and budget) the loss mode decided whether held-out facts were reached by step 16 000; with the answer-only loss they were reached at all three shares (budgets 4000–16 000; at 8.7 % only 1 of 4 runs by step 10 000, the budget of EXP-105), so in these explorations the share mainly changed when, not whether, the facts generalized. In these four trained language models (seed 3862; exploration, EXP-118), removing the Fourier subspace of the run's own five targets from the number-token rows lowered held-out fact accuracy from 0.95–0.99 to 0.03–0.30, while removing the other split's targets or any of four control sets left it at 0.93–0.99.

**Confirmation (LM1, frozen before any run, 10 fresh seeds; independently recomputed).** In the same 13.9 M-parameter language model (about 79 % arithmetic tokens, loss on the text and on the answer tokens, tied embeddings, 6000 steps), the engineered fact splits steered the number-token embedding on 10 fresh seeds: mean Δ_pow 5.32 [4.86, 5.73] at step 6000 (range 3.96–6.40), 10/10 seeds above the registered minimum effect 1 (exact sign-permutation p = 1/1024, the smallest attainable with 10 seeds, assuming symmetry about 1 under H0); held-out fact accuracy was ≥ 0.5 in 20/20 runs (0.898–0.995). This is one configuration with a high arithmetic share and an energy measure on tied embeddings; it does not show steering at a few percent arithmetic data (for that, see LM3 below) and does not by itself show functional use (tested next, LM1-F), and these P1-type splits also carry the targets in the number counts (see the equal-count splits below). **Confirmation of use (LM1-F, frozen before any ablation; independently recomputed, identical to within 2.2·10⁻⁸).** In the ten LM1 models, removing the Fourier subspace of the run's own five target frequencies from the number-token rows lowered held-out fact accuracy far more than removing the other split's targets (mean C̄ 0.882 [0.836, 0.918], above the registered minimum effect of 0.1; one-sided sign-permutation p = 1/1024, the resolution limit; 10 of 10 seeds with C̄ > 0): median held-out accuracy 0.982 at baseline, 0.048 without the own targets, 0.979 without the other split's targets and 0.978 without a single control set (median over the four control sets). With tied embeddings this ablation acts on input and output vectors alike, so it shows that the steered frequencies are used by the model, not whether the input or the output side uses them.

**Confirmation (LM3 and LM3-F, frozen before any run, 10 fresh seeds; independently recomputed).** With only about 8.7 % arithmetic tokens (two arithmetic sentences per sequence; loss on the text and on the answer tokens; tied embeddings; 15 000 steps), the engineered fact splits again steered the number-token embedding: mean Δ_pow 5.99 [4.64, 6.99] at step 15 000, 9 of 10 seeds above the registered minimum effect 1 (exact one-sided sign-permutation test against the minimum effect 1: p = 2/1024; these splits also carry the targets in the number counts); held-out fact accuracy was ≥ 0.5 in 20/20 runs (0.57–0.97). **When the steering appears (descriptive).** In LM1 (secondary measure), in 9 of 10 pairs the first snapshot with Δ_pow > 1 came before the first with training-fact accuracy ≥ 0.1 in both arms (8 by one snapshot of 250 steps, 1 by two), in 1 of 10 at the same snapshot (it did not precede the fit onset in 1 of 10). In both the language model and the Section 2 model, the steering became visible within one or two snapshots of the fit to the training pairs: in the Section 2 model (EXP-103, 50 seeds, post hoc) the first snapshot with Δ_pow > 1 and the first with training accuracy ≥ 0.1 in both arms had the same median (step 75); in the language model this onset came only after thousands of steps. In the Section 2 model this coincidence also held across a fourfold change of the learning rate at constant lr·wd (EXP-103, 10 paired seeds, post hoc: median fit onsets 29 to 92 steps, steering onsets later by a median factor of 1.10–1.17 in each setting), which by itself does not separate a dependence of the steering on the fit from a shared clock. *Figure K1* (`figures/fig14_lm_onset.py`, EXP-114, one F/G pair, exploration): Δ_pow on the number-token rows stays below 0.2 until step 1500, reaches 0.70 at 1750 and 3.62 at 2000, when the training-fact accuracy first exceeds 0.1 in both runs (0.80/0.70), and grows to 5.60 by step 4000; held-out fact accuracy follows the training facts with a short delay (0.98/0.99 at 4000).

**Counts and tied embeddings (explorations).** With tied embeddings the number rows also serve as output vectors, and in P1-type splits the counts of each number carry target structure, so a positive Δ_pow in a language model need not come from the pair structure alone; with exactly equal counts of every number in every role (EXP-104 splits, EXP-115, 2 seeds, exploration) the steering reached Δ_pow 5.23 and 1.83 at step 2500 and fell back to 4.35 and 0.96 by step 4000, so with equal counts one of two seeds kept Δ_pow well above 1 and the other ended just below it (pre-set reading 'unclear'; the F run of the second seed did not generalize, held-out accuracy 0.63). Post hoc (2 seeds), the fall is not a dilution: Δ_pow compares own-target with other-arm-target energy, so energy on other frequencies cannot lower it. Own-target energy still grew, but the energy on the other arm's targets grew faster in all four runs, most strongly in one run per seed, which carries most of the fall (×3.10 against ×1.39 and ×6.41 against ×3.13 between steps 2500 and 4000; also faster than the remaining frequencies, ×1.89 and ×2.78), whereas with P1-type splits the other arm's targets lost energy (×0.93 and ×0.89; EXP-114, 1 seed). The same flat splits in the toy model show no fall by the end of the common window (1 525 and 100 steps after both arms had generalized; exploration). With untied input and output embeddings (EXP-117, exploration, 2 seeds, P1-type splits, 6000 steps) the input rows of the number tokens were steered (Δ_in 6.44 and 5.54; held-out facts 0.92–0.97), so the effect is not only a property of shared output vectors; Δ_out of the output rows was also positive (4.04 and 4.74; descriptive). In these untied models, removing the run's own target subspace from the input rows alone lowered held-out accuracy as much as removing it from both sides (0.02–0.07 against 0.01–0.09), removing it from the output rows alone lowered it by 26–55 points, and removing the other split's targets left it at 0.92–0.97 on every side (single control sets 0.85–0.97; EXP-120, exploration on the same 4 models; pre-set reading "unclear" because one output-side contrast, 0.27, fell below the preset 0.3). Descriptively, not as a pre-set reading, the steered frequencies matter mainly on the input side and partly on the output side (the two projections are different interventions, so their sizes are not strictly comparable).

**Random splits and a commutativity plateau (explorations).** With the same trainer and tied embeddings (EXP-119, exploration, 2 seeds, 6000 steps), held-out facts from engineered splits reached accuracy 0.5 within 17–78 steps of the training facts, while a uniform random split of the same size was memorized equally early (training facts at 1870 and 2112 steps against 1872–2343) but did not reach 0.5 on held-out facts within 6000 steps (held-out accuracy stayed at about 0.3 from the end of memorization to step 6000, 0.33 and 0.32 at the end; pre-set reading "constructed faster", ratio q ≥ 3.15 and ≥ 2.61, censored). Descriptively (not pre-set; 2 seeds), the difference in the language model lies after memorization (training accuracy ≥ 0.5 was reached equally early), not in an earlier onset of learning. Trained longer (one run, seed 3866, 16 000 steps, exploration), the random split did not reach 0.5 on held-out facts (0.47 at step 16 000; pre-set reading 'unclear'), but the plateau was eroding: held-out facts without a trained mirror rose from 0.03 at step 6000 to 0.23 at step 16 000 (mirrored ones 1.00), so a later generalization is possible but was not observed. Post hoc (reading fixed before computing), the plateau consists of the held-out facts whose mirrored pair (b, a) was trained (0.99 and 1.00 correct, against 0.03 on the others; chance 0.009), whereas the engineered-split models answer both groups (0.97–1.00; 0.968 in one run): by step 6000 the model with the random split behaved as if it had learned the swap a + b = b + a (it answered correctly almost exactly where the mirrored fact was trained) and hardly any addition beyond it. That the composition rather than the amount of training facts sets the pace of grokking is known for implicit reasoning (Wang et al. 2024); here size and repetition are equal and only the choice of pairs differs, but that choice also puts the targets into the number counts, so a count effect is not excluded by this comparison alone (see the flat-count arm next and the count-matched control D in the next paragraph). A random split with exactly flat counts (EXP-121, exploration, 2 runs with the model seeds of the equal-count block above; every number 34 times in each role; its difference set was drawn at random, so no frequencies were chosen, but the construction still concentrates the mask spectrum on a few random frequencies: top-5 mask share 0.16, against 0.27–0.30 in the equal-count F/G splits, 0.17–0.19 in the engineered splits of EXP-119 and 0.105–0.107 in its random split) did not stall within 4000 steps: held-out facts reached 0.5 570 and 223 steps after the training facts (pre-set reading "unclear" against the preset bounds of 300 and 1000; the equal-count engineered splits of the same seeds, on a larger test set: 25–300), and post hoc (reading fixed before computing) the models also answered held-out facts without a trained mirror (0.68 and 0.98). Descriptively, in these two runs a choice of target frequencies was not needed to avoid the commutativity plateau. Uneven counts alone do not explain the plateau of the ordinary random split either, since the engineered splits of EXP-119 have equally uneven counts (coefficient of variation of the counts of a and b 0.13–0.16, random split 0.14–0.15) and generalized quickly; post hoc and across three blocks with different seeds and test sets, the splits that did not stay on the plateau all had a concentrated mask spectrum (by the largest single-frequency mask power, not the top-5 share: at least 1.6 times that of the two random splits; the cut was read off after the fact, and both plateau splits come from one block), which is consistent with the first-order picture that a spectral peak of the mask, wherever it lies, would be needed, but this was not tested directly.

**A commutativity stage before grokking (explorations with pre-set readings; pilot EXP-122 on 4 + 4 runs, main block EXP-123).** The language-model plateau above made us split held-out accuracy in the Section 2 transformer by whether the mirrored pair (b, a) of a held-out pair (a, b) was trained (about 30 % of held-out pairs in every split; this model has position embeddings, so commutativity is not built in). On the random splits of the 20 R5 units (same initializations and test sets as Section 4, whose generalization times were known; readings, code and thresholds fixed before the runs; disclosures in the EXP-123 record), held-out pairs with a trained mirror reached accuracy 0.5 at least 100 steps before the other held-out pairs (or while these stayed below 0.5 up to 8000 steps, 1 unit) in 18 of 18 units where this was determinable (one-sided sign test, Holm-p 8·10⁻⁶; the same 18 of 18 at thresholds 0.3 and 0.7, post hoc; in the two remaining units the mirrored pairs never reached 0.5, and neither unit generalized within 8000 steps). So on random splits the test accuracy is first carried by held-out pairs whose mirror was trained, consistent with a learned symmetry a + b = b + a (median 4150 steps to 0.5 on mirrored pairs over the 18 units that reached it), and the other pairs follow in a late, steep jump (median 5325 steps over the 17 units that reached 0.5; Figure K2). He et al. (2026a) report the corresponding split of test accuracy at the end of memorization in a two-layer network whose input h_a + h_b makes it commutative by construction; here the symmetry has to be learned. The pre-set hypothesis that engineered splits mainly shorten the second stage was not supported: when the mirrored pairs first reached 0.5, the other pairs were further on engineered than on random splits by a mean of only 0.041 [0.020, 0.062], below the pre-set minimum effect of 0.05 (14 of 18 units; sign-permutation p = 0.001), and post hoc, in absolute steps, engineered splits shortened the commutativity stage more (geometric mean ×11.1 over 17 units) than the remaining stage (×4.6). The count-matched control D behaved like the random split in this measure (difference in the other pairs' accuracy at the mirrored pairs' 0.5 crossing 0.003, 11 of 17 units, p = 0.40), although fewer D units generalized (14 of 20 against 16 of 20). Post hoc (session 57, readings fixed before computing), the mirrored pairs crossed 0.5 at a similar embedding concentration in all three arms: at the last spectrum snapshot at or before the crossing (every 250 steps; for 6 engineered units this is step 0) the five strongest frequencies carried a median 0.31 of the W_E power on random splits (18 units), 0.32 on control D (19) and 0.29 on engineered splits (20), and 0.33, 0.33 and 0.38 at the first snapshot after it, against 0.10 at initialization and medians of 0.49–0.58 at generalization (generalizing units only: 16, 14 and 20); per-unit values vary widely (0.10–0.46). Engineered splits reached this point earlier (median 325 against 4150 steps), although mirrored pairs were equally frequent in all arms (0.30). Within the random and the control arm, the first snapshot with a concentration of at least 0.30 tracked the crossing across units (Spearman 0.76 and 0.81), but it tracked generalization more closely (on the same generalizing units, 16 and 14: 0.65 and 0.59 for the crossing against 0.90 and 0.85 for generalization), so the concentration may mark overall progress rather than the symmetry; this is a covariation, not evidence that the concentration produces the symmetry. So in the toy model the engineered splits shorten both stages, the first more in absolute steps (post hoc), whereas the two random-split language-model runs stalled in the second; the two settings need not share a mechanism. All units come from the R5 block, so this is not an independent confirmation.

*Figure K2* (`figures/fig15_commutativity_stages.py`, EXP-123, 20 R5 units per arm, exploration): held-out accuracy on pairs whose mirrored pair was trained (solid) and on the others (dashed) against training step divided by the run's generalization step; thin lines are units, thick lines medians over the units that generalized (16/20 random, 14/20 count-matched control D, 20/20 engineered E2); units that did not generalize are drawn against 8000 steps. **Context.** Pretrained language models compute addition with Fourier features of the number tokens (Zhou, Fu, Sharan and Jia 2024) or a helix (Kantamneni and Tegmark 2025) and carry circular features for periodic concepts such as weekdays and months (Engels et al. 2024); models trained from scratch on addition use mainly low frequencies (Zhou, Fu, Sharan and Jia 2024), and data, tokenizer and architecture shape which periods appear (Fu et al. 2026). These works describe which number features models end up with; the explorations in this appendix ask whether the choice of arithmetic facts prescribes them. In our searches (2026-09-28 and 2026-09-29) we did not find this question addressed for a model trained from scratch; this is provisional.
