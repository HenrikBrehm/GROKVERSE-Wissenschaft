## Abstract (Entwurf, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

Networks that grok modular arithmetic use a sparse set of Fourier features, and which frequencies a network uses is usually treated as an outcome of its initialization. We show that the composition of the training set steers this choice. In pre-registered replications on fresh seeds, engineered training splits enrich five prescribed frequencies in the embedding of a one-layer transformer for addition mod 113 and 97, multiplication mod 113 and the dihedral group D59 (50 seeds per task), and equal-norm embedding perturbations along the steered frequencies reduce the logit margin more than along controls (20 seeds per task; D59 after grokking). In addition mod 113, even an ordinary uniform 30 % split biases the learned frequencies, though far from determining them, and engineered splits generalize in about 6.5× fewer steps than uniform random splits and 7.5× fewer than a control with the same number of pairs per output (geometric means, times bounded at 8000 steps; all 20 units in the same direction). The choice starts to persist within 25 to 50 full-batch steps (addition mod 113, learning rate 10⁻³): the sign of an ablation contrast between old and new target frequencies reverses between a data switch after 25 and after 50 steps (it tracks embedding energy), and a switch after 25 steps delays grokking by a factor of about 1.8, whereas a switch to new pairs with the same target frequencies costs no time on average. All of these results use full-batch training. Under i.i.d. minibatches (batch 256 and 64) the steering was confirmed on fresh seeds; a fixed data order did not override it at 30 % data (pilot), and under i.i.d. minibatches of 256 the choice starts to persist later in steps (half-point 114 steps on 10 fresh seeds, energy measure). We report effect sizes with confidence intervals, the limits of each measure (functional sensitivity follows spectral energy), and negative results.

## 1 Introduction (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43, Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung; Quelle: Langfassung §1, §1b und [[paper/related_work]])

Small networks trained on modular arithmetic first memorize their training pairs and only much later generalize ("grokking"; Power et al. 2022). The generalizing solution computes with a handful of Fourier frequencies of the group (Nanda et al. 2023), or with a few irreducible representations for other finite groups (Chughtai et al. 2023). *Which* frequencies a particular network uses has been described as arbitrary across runs (Chughtai et al. 2023); many frequency sets implement the task equally well (Gromov 2023), and at a fixed training set, hyperparameters and initialization can even change the type of algorithm (Zhong et al. 2023). Recent theory attributes the competition between frequencies to the initialization (He et al. 2026a). For uniform data it derives the order of learned representations from the Fourier statistics of the input encoding (Marchetti et al. 2026). With one-hot tokens that encoding favours no frequency in cyclic groups and ties irreducible representations of equal dimension, and the role of the train–test split has been named as open (He et al. 2026b).

We ask whether the training set itself can decide this choice. In the one-layer transformer of a previous grokking study, with fixed one-hot tokens and full-batch AdamW, we construct training splits whose pair mask concentrates its spectrum on five prescribed frequencies. This lever differs from three known ones. LeDoux (2026) showed that a fixed, structured order of examples sets the fundamental frequency under minibatch training, with the same frequency across random training subsets. Marchetti et al. (2026) work through encoding statistics. In recursive feature machines, training sets that are invariant under a subgroup of the task's symmetry group (for example, with the fixed points of a reflection removed) prevent generalization, and the learned feature matrix then encodes only that subgroup; breaking the symmetry of the training set restores generalization (Tomàs Bernal et al. 2026; kernels only, symmetric splits, no prescribed frequencies). Here only *which* pairs are shown changes, the split has no built-in symmetry, and the targets are prescribed frequencies. In language models and related architectures, data, architecture, optimizer and tokenizer decide whether periodic number features become geometrically separable (Fu et al. 2026). For word embeddings, co-occurrence symmetry explains circular and other low-dimensional structures in theory, matched empirically in language models (Karkada et al. 2026). Our setting isolates the data lever in a task where the learned algorithm is known.

We report three pre-registered results with fresh seeds and frozen analysis manifests. (1) Engineered splits steer the learned frequencies in four tasks, including a non-abelian group, and networks are more sensitive along the steered frequencies; an ordinary random split also biases the choice, though far from determining it (Section 3; sensitivity follows spectral energy). (2) Engineered splits generalize several times faster than uniform random splits and than a control with the same number of pairs per output (Section 4). (3) The choice starts to persist within 25 to 50 full-batch steps (at learning rate 10⁻³). A switch after 25 steps delays grokking by about ×1.8, one after 50 steps does not (it even reaches the threshold earlier than training on the destination split alone), and a switch to new pairs with the same target frequencies costs no time on average (Section 5). That early training can resist later data is familiar from critical learning periods (Achille et al. 2019), the primacy bias (Nikishin et al. 2022) and warm starting (Ash and Adams 2020). In fine-tuning, fixed-clock optimizer memory makes data-order effects first order in the learning rate (Sweeney 2026). What we add is a measured time scale of tens of steps for a prescribed feature choice under a controlled data switch.

Because LeDoux's order effect arises under minibatch training, Section 6 (L0) tests whether the steering survives minibatch training and a structured example order that favours other frequencies. All claims hold for one model family and for constructed splits; R4, R5 and Section 5 hold for addition mod 113 only, except the persistence window in embedding energy (also addition mod 97 and multiplication mod 113, R11). Explorations, resets, the optimizer clock of the lock-in, and a first-order account are in the appendix. Novelty statements mean "not found in our search", not "new".

## 2 Setup (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43, Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung; Quelle: Langfassung §2)

**Model and training.** One-layer transformer of the original GROKVERSE study (following Nanda et al. 2023, without biases): embedding W_E and positional embedding (d_model = 128), one attention layer with four heads of width 32, a ReLU MLP of width 512, no layer normalization, untied readout. Inputs are (a, b, =); the loss is the cross-entropy at the last position. Training is full-batch AdamW (learning rate 10⁻³, weight decay 1, β = (0.9, 0.98)) on 30 % of all pairs. The two arms of a seed share the initialization; only the training set differs. Confirmation blocks ran on one GPU after a statistical equivalence check against the CPU path; runs compared within a block used the same device.

**Tasks.** Addition mod 113 (primary) and 97, multiplication mod 113 (frequencies in the discrete-logarithm coordinate, Z_112; pairs with a zero operand are not in the group and keep uniform weight in the split construction), and composition in the dihedral group D59 (order 118; the 29 two-dimensional irreducible representations).

**Engineered splits.** Per seed, two disjoint target sets of five frequencies each. Arm F draws 30 % of all pairs without replacement with weights q(a, b) = max(0.05, 1 + ε·w(a, b)), w(a, b) = (1/5) Σ_{k∈T_F} cos(2πk·u(a)/n + φ_k(c)) with the group coordinate u(a), the output c and a random phase per target and output (for D59 an irrep trace); arm G likewise; ε = 2. Token counts are no more unequal than in a random split (coefficient of variation 0.14 in the pilot construction), although their spectrum is also target-enriched (Appendix). The main difference lies in the spectrum of the pair mask (centred target share 0.194 against 0.089 for a random split).

**Switch protocol (Result 3; addition mod 113).** A common held-out test set (20 %) is removed first; two pair-disjoint training sets A and B of 3831 pairs pick their pairs in alternation from their own weighted orders (steering strength calibrated to a mask target share of 0.185). A run trains t steps on A, switches to B (optimizer state kept) and trains a fixed further budget; each seed runs both directions.

**Measures.** On the group-element rows X of W_E, P_k(X) is the power in the Fourier (irrep) subspace of k. *Steering*: Δ_pow = log[P_F(T_F)/P_F(T_G)] + log[P_G(T_G)/P_G(T_F)] at step 2000. *Margin sensitivity*: the drop of the mean correct-class logit margin on pairs outside both splits after a step X − δ·u_S, δ = 0.02‖X‖, along the arm's own target set S (u_S = P_S X/‖P_S X‖, the normalized projection of X onto the frequency (irrep) subspaces of S), minus the mean drop along four control sets (drawn without both target sets and the harmonics 2k), averaged over both arms. *Persistence*: π = (L_switch − L_pureB)/(L_pureA − L_pureB), L = log[P(T_A)/P(T_B)] of the final embedding, averaged over both switch directions (an unbounded affine index, not a probability). *Ablation contrast*: accuracy after removing the new target subspaces from W_E minus accuracy after removing the old ones. *Time to generalization*: first evaluation with test accuracy ≥ 0.95, evaluated every 25 steps and bounded at a fixed horizon: in R5 a stable event (≥ 0.95 at s, s + 25 and s + 50 after train accuracy ≥ 0.99), bounded at 8000 steps; in R6/R8 the first crossing counted from step 0, bounded at 2000, compared as log2 ratios with the destination-only run.

**Protocol.** Every confirmation block was registered before its runs: hypotheses, primary measure, minimum effect, family with Holm correction, sample size, a reserved block of fresh seeds, and the previously seen data with the decisions they influenced. A committed manifest stores code hashes, and the analysis accepts only runs bound to it (Appendix H). Intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over seeds. Pilots are reported separately as explorations. Blocks are named by short identifiers (P1, R4, …); Table 1 maps each identifier to its hypothesis, manifest commit, fresh seeds and number of runs (generated from the frozen manifests, `figures/table_registrations.py`).

## 3 Result 1: the composition of the training set steers the learned frequencies (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**Steering (P1, pre-registered replication on 50 fresh seeds per task; tasks chosen as steerable in pilots).** For each seed we built two engineered splits F and G of the same size whose pair masks concentrate their spectrum on five disjoint target frequencies (irreducible representations for D59), and trained the same initialization on each. Only the split differs, so the comparison is a paired intervention. At step 2000 the relative W_E energy of the own against the other targets, Δ_pow, was 8.24 [7.92, 8.54] for addition mod 113, 8.09 [7.75, 8.43] for multiplication mod 113, 6.11 [5.79, 6.43] for addition mod 97 and 2.64 [2.55, 2.73] for D59, positive in 50/50 seeds in every task (Holm-p 1.5·10⁻⁵, worst case; Figure 1). Descriptively, the five own targets carried on average 0.80 of the embedding's group power in addition mod 113 (chance 0.089), 0.79 in multiplication mod 113, 0.69 in addition mod 97 and 0.44 in D59 (chance 0.17). For D59, step 2000 is before grokking; after grokking (P2f, 12 000 steps) the own irreducible representations carried a median 0.98 of the energy. In the other three tasks the steering is not a pre-grokking transient: in separate stored runs (P2d/P2e, 20 other seeds per task, median generalization at step 725, 1225 for addition mod 97; post hoc exploration, not P1) Δ_pow stayed positive in every seed at every stored step and grew after generalization before levelling off without falling back, e.g. 7.77 at step 2000 and 9.44 [8.99, 9.90] at step 4000 for addition mod 113. The steering also held when one hyperparameter deviated (R1; weakest where few runs had generalized by step 2000), in a shared-embedding MLP (at 10 000 steps, after grokking), at 4× width, in a two-layer transformer (smaller: −2.70 [−3.16, −2.18] against one layer; R7), and for non-modular integer addition, where no network generalized, so there it is a property of the representation only (P4). Details in Appendix A.

**Functional sensitivity (P2d, P2e, P2f).** A step of norm 0.02·‖X‖ (X = number rows of W_E) along the steered frequencies reduced the mean correct-class logit margin on pairs outside both splits more than steps along four pre-specified control sets: 0.250 [0.229, 0.270] (addition mod 113, 4000 steps), 0.291 [0.268, 0.308] (multiplication mod 113, 8000 steps), 0.294 [0.275, 0.312] (addition mod 97, 8000 steps), each 20/20 seeds, and D = 0.233 [0.214, 0.253] for D59 after grokking (12 000 steps, 20/20). In the addition-mod-113 switch models where we tested it (not the P2d–P2f models), this sensitivity followed the energy distribution: at equal removed energy the dominant set had no advantage. We therefore claim sensitivity, not use beyond energy. Three earlier registered functional variants were not evaluable, aborted or not confirmed; they are reported in Appendix B.

**An ordinary random split also shapes the choice (R4, addition mod 113, 60 fresh units).** Each unit crossed two initializations with two uniform 30 % splits (8000 steps). Networks sharing a split had more similar embedding spectra than networks sharing nothing: after 8000 steps, Δ_split = 0.118 [0.079, 0.157] in an energy-weighted cosine (48/60 units positive), and before grokking the rank order of all 56 frequencies was also more alike (Spearman at step 2000, 0.33 [0.30, 0.36]). Descriptively the shared split raised similarity more than a shared initialization, but it is far from determining the choice: networks with the same split share on average about one of their five strongest frequencies, against about one half without a shared split (chance 25/56 ≈ 0.45). This concerns representation energy, not use (no ablation), in one task and one architecture. Post hoc, the five strongest frequencies of a network rank higher in its own split's coherent mask spectrum (M0c) than in the other split's, already at step 250 and not at initialization (exploration, Appendix M). The engineered splits of Result 1 are thus consistent with a stronger dose of an effect that already acts on ordinary random splits.

**What this shows and what it does not.** In a paired design with engineered splits, the choice of training pairs enriches the prescribed frequencies in four tasks, with fixed one-hot tokens and full-batch training. Section 6 (L0) tests whether the steering survives minibatch training and a competing order signal. LeDoux (2026) reports the same frequency across random training subsets under a structured example order at p = 9973, plausibly a different regime (not measured) with ≈ 80× more pairs, in which the spectral fluctuations of a uniform random subset, shrinking roughly as 1/√n, are about 9× smaller.

## 4 Result 2: engineered splits generalize earlier (R5; Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**Design (pre-registered, 20 fresh units, addition mod 113, 8000 steps).** Per unit, seven training sets of 3831 pairs share one held-out test set and one initialization: a uniform random split R, engineered splits with ε = 2 for two target sets and ε = 1 and 8 for one of them, and for each ε = 2 split a control D with exactly the same number of training pairs per output value (per anti-diagonal), but with positions drawn uniformly within each anti-diagonal. The event is a stable rise of test accuracy to ≥ 0.95 after train accuracy ≥ 0.99; times are bounded at 8000 steps.

**Result.** The engineered split generalized in ×6.5 [5.3, 7.9] fewer steps than the random split (target set A) and ×7.5 [6.5, 8.6] fewer than the matched control (mean over both target sets; geometric means of bounded time ratios; 20/20 units in the same direction for both; Holm-p 2·10⁻⁵; Figure 2). Higher ε was faster (ε = 1 took ×14.4 [12.4, 16.6] as long as ε = 8; secondary, outside the family; ε changes pair selection and truncation together, so this is not by itself evidence for concentration). Median bounded times were 775–813 steps (ε = 2), 6163 (R) and 6850–7513 (D). By step 8000, 16/20 random-split units and 14/20 and 11/20 control units had generalized; the others count at the bound, so the ratios are conservative.

**What this shows and what it does not.** At matched per-output counts, the arrangement of the pairs within the anti-diagonals, which carries the spectral concentration of the mask, accounts for the speed-up relative to D; the output marginals alone do not produce it. D matches only the frozen statistics, so finer geometric differences are not excluded. "Earlier" means an earlier stable rise within 8000 steps, not a higher final accuracy. Known levers on the grokking delay include the initialization scale and weight norm (Kumar et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2023; Khanh et al. 2026), and that early task-aligned embedding structure shortens it is known from other interventions (Howe 2026b; GrokTransfer). The contribution here is that the choice of training pairs alone produces it, at matched size, initialization and test set. Post hoc, a higher early target share goes with earlier generalization; whether the speed-up runs entirely through this early signal is open. Limits: one task, one architecture and optimizer setting (lr 10⁻³, weight decay 1), train fraction 30 %, ε = 2 for the primary contrasts.

## 5 Result 3: the choice starts to persist within 25–50 full-batch steps (K4, K4c, R6, R8; addition mod 113; Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**Persistence after a switch (P3, N28; pre-registered, 30 fresh seeds each).** After 25 steps on a first engineered split and 2000 steps on a pair-disjoint second split, final models sat at 0.351 of the normalized log-energy distance between the two pure endpoints (persistence π), 0.293 [0.263, 0.322] above any tested static additive trace (30/30), i.e. above the π of the pure-destination embedding plus c times the embedding change of the first 25 steps, maximized over c ∈ [0, 1]. Zeroing Adam's first moment at the switch lowered π (separate block N28, 0.340 → 0.189), and ten extra pre-switch steps compensated this, compatible with an "exposure" reading (Appendix M); that the first moment carries such a signal is known from other settings (Sweeney 2026; Xu 2026).

**An ablation contrast reverses (N29, 20 fresh seeds).** Removing all five target frequencies from W_E after a switch and 4000 further steps: after a switch at step 50, removing the old targets cost 0.903 [0.846, 0.953] more test accuracy than removing the new ones (20/20 seeds above 0.3); after a switch at step 25, removing the new targets cost 0.603 [0.498, 0.700] more (17/20). The embedding energy points the same way (post hoc), so the contrast does not separate reliance from energy. The two time points bracket a reversal; a network-wide or irreversible commitment is not shown.

**A switch inside this window costs time (R6, 30 fresh seeds).** With b the first step with test accuracy ≥ 0.95 (bounded at 2000) and C(t) the log2 time ratio against training on the destination split only, a switch at step 25 delayed grokking by C(25) = +0.85 [0.73, 0.96] (a factor ≈ 1.8; 29/30 seeds; Figure 3) and more than a switch at step 50 (C(25) − C(50) = +1.25 [0.97, 1.50]; 28/30). Across the grid (t = 10, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 75), the mean cost was positive at t = 10–40 (at 40 only +0.27, 22/30) and negative at 50 and 75. C(t) compares regimes: t destination updates are replaced by source updates, no mixed A∪B control was run, and time to threshold is evaluated every 25 steps. All 60 destination-only runs generalized by step 2000; switch runs that had not (4–14 of 30 per direction at t = 10–30, at most 2 at t ≥ 40) count at the bound, so C(t) underestimates the delay where it is largest. The confirmed difference between 25 and 50 argues against an exposure-only reading, which predicts C(50) ≥ C(25).

**Only a change of targets costs (R8, 25 fresh seeds).** A switch at step 25 to a pair-disjoint split with the same five targets and matched steering strength did not delay grokking on average (−0.15 log2; 8/25 seeds slower), whereas the switch to other targets did (+0.66; 24/25); difference +0.81 [0.57, 1.04]. The delay is therefore not a generic cost of an early change of training pairs, nor of fewer destination updates alone. Because the same-target split has partly reversed wave phases, a pure phase conflict is not excluded. Several switch runs had not generalized by step 2000 (A→B 20/25, B→A 15/25 did), so C_AB is bounded. One task, one architecture.

**The window generalizes beyond addition mod 113 (R11, the same 30 fresh seeds for both tasks; Sitzung 46, independently recomputed).** With the EXP-044 protocol (switch F → G at 25 or 50 steps, one direction, then 2000 steps; π from final embedding energy), the first split's choice persisted mostly after 50 but not after 25 steps also in addition mod 97 (π(25) = 0.38, π(50) = 0.90; difference +0.52 [0.45, 0.59], 30/30 seeds) and in multiplication mod 113, which in discrete-log coordinates is addition in Z₁₁₂ plus a zero token (0.27 and 0.74; +0.47 [0.40, 0.54], 30/30). All six registered tests held (Holm); the weakest was that the second split wins at 25 steps in addition mod 97 (0.5 − π(25) = +0.12 [0.07, 0.18], 23/30). This is the embedding-energy measure only; the ablation contrast and the time cost were not re-tested there.

## 6 L0: set composition against data order under minibatch training (Kurzfassung; gekürzt in Sitzung 48, der bisherige Volltext mit allen Zahlen steht unverändert in Anhang J unten)

**Question.** All results above use full-batch training. LeDoux (2026) found, under minibatch training with a fixed, structured example order, the same fundamental frequency across random training subsets. We ask (i) whether the steering by the set survives i.i.d. minibatch training, (ii) what happens when a fixed order favours other frequencies than the set, and (iii) when the choice starts to persist under minibatches.

**Design.** Addition mod 113, the setup of Section 2, AdamW with i.i.d. minibatches of 256 or 64 pairs; a pilot (EXP-100, 8 seeds) also ran full batch with a matched 30 000-step budget and SGD with momentum. The conflict arm uses a fixed stride order after LeDoux, pairs sorted by (a mod 10, a) and identical in every epoch, which puts its spectral weight on the frequencies O = {11, 12, 22, 23, 34, 45}; the targets of the engineered splits exclude O. A first-order computation made before any run (Appendix J) predicted that at 30 % data the order can compete with the set within the full-batch lock-in window only for long epochs (a necessary condition only; the minibatch window turned out later, below), so an arm with batch 16 (240 steps per epoch) was added before any run; it uses weight decay 0.1, because weight decay 1 did not generalize there in 30 000 steps (a regime change). LeDoux's order effect was strong at 0.3 % data (≈ 1165 steps per epoch); our regime is far from it. Readings were fixed before the runs; the conflict is judged only if the order is shown to act at all.

**Steering survives minibatch training (confirmed).** On 10 fresh seeds (L0, frozen before any run), Δ_pow was 9.43 [9.06, 9.81] at batch 256 and 9.60 [8.79, 10.46] at batch 64 (10/10 seeds each; Holm-p 4·10⁻¹² and 3·10⁻⁹; Figure 4a). In the pilot, the same batch sizes gave 9.60 and 9.85 against 10.28 for full batch with the same budget. At batch 64, 12 of 20 confirmation runs had not settled at the end of the budget (final train accuracy below 0.99), so the claim is steering at the end of the budget, not in converged networks. The steering also held at batch 16 with weight decay 0.1 (pilot EXP-106, 8 seeds: 9.21 [8.82, 9.60]).

**Order against set (pilots, exploration).** At batch 256 and 64 the stride order did not shift the learned frequencies (peak frequency in O in 0/16 runs each; order-share difference on the engineered splits +0.004 [−0.003, 0.015] and +0.005 [−0.000, 0.011]), so by the pre-set rule the conflict was not testable there. At batch 16 the conflict was again not testable by the pre-set rule (order-alone reading 'unclear'). Descriptively, the order raised the order share on random splits (+0.16 [0.07, 0.24]; about 0 in the three seeds whose i.i.d. run already carried 0.20–0.24 on O) but not on the engineered splits, where it only delayed generalization (0.67 log2 [0.42, 0.93], ≈ ×1.6, 15/16 pairs). This is consistent with LeDoux's remark that the importance of order scales inversely with the content signal of the data. SGD with momentum could not be calibrated and is not evaluable.

**The lock-in window under minibatches (confirmed).** Under i.i.d. minibatches of 256, a switch after 25 or 50 steps left no persistent trace (pilot, 8 seeds: π ≈ 0, against π(50) = 0.87 in full batch on the same seeds). The window lies later in steps and earlier in epochs (pilots EXP-107/108): the half-point is near 133 steps (8.9 epochs) at batch 256, against about 31 steps (31 epochs) in full batch (linear interpolation, energy measure). On 10 fresh seeds (N54b, frozen before any run, 840 runs) the half-point t½ at lr 10⁻³ was 58, 114 and 289 steps at batch 1024, 256 and 64; it grew with the steps per epoch N/B (Figure 4b, c) with a mean log–log slope α = 0.583 [0.560, 0.606] (registered square-root band [0.3, 0.7]; Holm-p 5·10⁻⁶). Doubling the learning rate raised the slope in every seed (Δα = +0.139 [0.098, 0.178], 10/10, Holm-p 6·10⁻⁵): it shortened lock-in to 0.58 [0.54, 0.62] of its duration at batch 1024 but only to 0.85 [0.78, 0.92] at batch 64. log t½ is curved (two-point slopes 0.48 and 0.68 at lr 10⁻³), so α is an average slope over N/B = 3.7–60, not a clean power law. In our search (29 September 2026) we did not find a prior measurement of a square-root batch-size scaling of an early lock-in time at fixed learning rate. The pilots, a model comparison fixed before the N54b results and all limits are in Appendix J.

## 7 Discussion and limitations (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**What the three results say together.** In small networks trained full-batch on these tasks, which pairs are shown steers which frequencies the embedding favours, strongly for engineered splits and weakly for random ones (Result 1; measured as embedding energy). In addition mod 113 and one architecture, engineered splits also generalize several times faster at fixed size (Result 2). In the same setting, full-batch and at the default learning rate and weight decay, the choice starts to persist within 25 to 50 steps, as measured by embedding energy and by an ablation contrast that does not separate reliance from energy (Result 3). None of this requires changing the tokens, the architecture or the loss.

**Limitations.** (i) *Regime:* unless stated otherwise, results use full-batch AdamW (lr 10⁻³, weight decay 1, β = 0.9/0.98) and a train fraction of 30 %. R1 varies one hyperparameter at a time for the steering. The reversal of Result 3 held at double learning rate but its switching side was not confirmed at 0.3× weight decay (Appendix, R2). Minibatch training and data order are tested only in L0 (Section 6): steering survived i.i.d. minibatches of 256 and 64 (energy measure, at the end of the budget), a conflict between set and a fixed order was not testable at 30 % data under the pre-set rule, and the lock-in took more steps at smaller batches (N54b, energy measure), so the 25–50-step figure of Result 3 is a full-batch result. (ii) *Scope:* steering holds in four tasks; other architectures (MLP, 4× width, two layers) and functional sensitivity were each tested in fewer settings (Appendix). R4, R5 and Result 3 hold for addition mod 113 in one architecture only, except that the persistence window of Result 3 (energy measure) was also confirmed for addition mod 97 and multiplication mod 113 (R11). (iii) *Measures:* functional sensitivity follows spectral energy in the addition-mod-113 switch models where this was tested (not in the P2d–P2f models). We make no claim about use beyond energy. Resets (Appendix) localize sensitivity, not mechanism. (iv) *Time scales* are counted in optimizer steps. The persistence does not follow the step count alone (a descriptive reading of R3; its Λ clock was not confirmed). With Adam's moment windows rescaled to summed learning rate, it followed summed learning rate in two confirmed cells (R9, addition mod 113, π measure; Appendix). Under minibatches the learning-rate effect depended on the batch size (N54b), so no single summed-learning-rate clock holds there. (v) *Horizon:* switch experiments are read at most ≈ 5000 steps after the switch (full batch 2000–4000), other final models at 2000–12 000 steps; much longer training is untested, and representation metrics measured at grokking need not be converged; for example, effective rank kept compressing for tens of thousands of steps after generalization (Khanh 2026b). (vi) *Design history:* many decisions were informed by pilots on seeds that were already used, and each registration lists them. Every confirmation used fresh reserved seeds and a frozen analysis manifest or code gate. Post hoc statements (e.g. energy direction, scalar Adam estimate, equal-energy test) are not confirmations.

## Anhang J (Kurzfassung): L0 und die Minibatch-Uhr der Festlegung im Detail (bisheriger Text von §6, in Sitzung 48 unverändert hierher verschoben)

**Question.** All results above use full-batch training. LeDoux (2026) found, under minibatch training with a fixed, structured example order, the same fundamental frequency across random training subsets. We ask whether the steering by the set survives minibatch training, and what happens when an order favours a different frequency than the set.

**Design (pilot EXP-100; the confirmation follows as its own registration).** Addition mod 113, the setup of Section 2. Per seed, engineered splits F and G whose five targets exclude the frequencies favoured by the order, and a uniform random split R. Arms: full batch for 2000 and for 30 000 steps (a budget-matched reference); i.i.d. minibatches of 256 and of 64 pairs (AdamW); SGD with momentum 0.9 (batch 256; learning rate calibrated beforehand on separate runs); and a fixed stride order after LeDoux, pairs sorted by (a mod ⌊√p⌋, a) = (a mod 10, a), identical in every epoch (batch 256). The stride order puts its spectral weight on frequency 11 (49 %), then 23, 34, 12, 45 and 22 (O); the sets carry no more weight on O than chance. The random split with i.i.d. and with stride order measures the order effect alone. A feasibility step before the pilot tests whether the stride order acts at all at 30 % data, at batch 256, 64 and 16; if it acts only at a smaller batch, the conflict arm is also run there (with i.i.d. and stride arms at that batch size), and if it acts at neither, the conflict is reported as not testable at 30 % data, as fixed before the runs. Rationale, computed before any run from the split files (first order: no optimizer, no weight decay; at most a necessary condition): under full-batch training the frequency choice starts to persist within the first 25–50 steps (Result 3), and summed over whole epochs the stride batches reproduce the set. On the random split (mean over 8 seeds), the excess order-frequency share of the summed batch masks over i.i.d. batches is 0.005 after 50 steps at batch 256 (15 steps per epoch); at batch 64 (60 steps per epoch) it is 0.147 after 25 steps, 0.047 after 50 steps and 0 after one full epoch. The value at 50 steps lies at the edge of our criterion, half of the engineered split's target excess (0.5 × 0.094 = 0.047; 2 of 8 seeds fall below it), so whether the order can compete within the lock-in window at batch 64 is open and is decided empirically. LeDoux's order effect was strong at ≈ 1165 steps per epoch (only the stride order generalized) and modest at about 10 steps per epoch (p = 97, 26.5 % data; fixed orders ≈ 20 % faster); our regime at batch 256 lies on the modest side. At batch 16 (240 steps per epoch) the same first-order computation gives an order excess of 0.194 after 50 steps, twice the set's target excess, and only 0.21 epochs fit into the lock-in window, so even i.i.d. batches carry less of the set's spectrum early on (early target share 0.10 against 0.18 at batch 256); batch 16 was added to the feasibility step for this reason, before any run.

**Readings (fixed before the runs).** Steering under minibatch: Δ_pow (as in Section 3) with confidence intervals per arm, also at equal step count (2000). Conflict: "set wins", "order wins" or "both act", judged only if the order is shown to act (otherwise "not testable"); measured by Δ_pow under the stride order, the order frequencies' share of the embedding energy, and the peak frequency. Speed: time to generalization in steps and epochs, engineered against random split.

**Result (pilot EXP-100, 8 seeds, exploration; Sitzung 46; all numbers independently recomputed).** With i.i.d. minibatches of 256 and of 64 pairs, the engineered splits still steer the learned frequencies (Δ_pow 9.60 [8.98, 10.21] and 9.85 [9.00, 10.73] at the end of 30 000 steps, against 10.28 [9.24, 11.21] for full batch with the same budget); the steering of Result 1 is not a full-batch artefact in this setting. As predicted by the first-order dilution estimate (which assumed the full-batch window; see below), it is weaker at batch 64 at equal step count (at step 2000: full batch 8.36, batch 256 8.05, batch 64 7.29); at the end of the budget this difference is gone. At 30 % data a fixed stride order did not shift the learned frequencies at either batch size (peak frequency in O in 0/16 runs at each; order-share difference on the engineered splits +0.004 [−0.003, 0.015] and +0.005 [−0.000, 0.011]), as a first-order estimate predicted for batch 256 (hypothesis: an epoch of 15 steps is shorter than the lock-in window); for batch 64 it predicted an edge case, and the feasibility runs showed an order effect there only through one seed (+0.05), the pilot none; the order effect reported by LeDoux (2026) at 0.3 % data (an epoch of ≈ 1165 steps) does not appear in this regime. On a random split the evidence for an order effect is intermediate (peak in O in 0/8 and 1/8 stride runs, but an order-share difference whose CI lies below 0, driven by i.i.d. runs (4 of 8 seeds at batch 256, order share up to 0.57) whose random split itself favours O); we report it as unclear. SGD with momentum could not be calibrated (no learning rate generalized in both calibration seeds) and is not evaluable. Engineered splits generalized ≈ 4.3× faster than random splits also under minibatches at batch 256 (median 77 against 333 epochs; pilot only, descriptive, not part of the confirmation). **Limits:** pilot seeds; at batch 64 many runs were not settled at the end (final train accuracy below 0.99 in 7/16 i.i.d. and 14/16 stride runs), so its end values are read partly from unstable networks. At batch 16 (240 steps per epoch) the network did not generalize with the default weight decay 1 within 30 000 steps (3 feasibility seeds, random split; test accuracy ≤ 0.73); with weight decay 0.1 it learns, and the order-against-set test in this long-epoch regime (EXP-106) and the confirmation on fresh seeds are reported below. **The lock-in window under minibatch (pilot, 8 seeds, switch at 25 or 50 steps, both directions).** The full-batch reference replicated on these seeds (π(25) = 0.38, π(50) = 0.87; difference +0.49 [0.39, 0.59]). Under i.i.d. minibatches of 256, the early lock-in of Result 3 did not appear by 50 steps: the choice does not yet persist after 50 steps (π(25) = −0.01, π(50) = −0.02; difference −0.00 [−0.05, 0.05]). Post hoc, at an equal horizon (step 2000) π was also ≈ 0, so this is not an effect of the longer training after the switch. At batch 256, 50 steps are 3.3 epochs, against 50 epochs in full batch; a follow-up (EXP-107, same 8 pilot seeds, switch at 100–1500 steps, exploration) found the window later in steps and earlier in epochs: π(100) = 0.33 [0.24, 0.42], π(200) = 0.85 [0.78, 0.92], π ≈ 1.00 from 375 steps (25 epochs) on; the half-point lies near 133 steps (8.9 epochs) against about 31 steps (31 epochs) in full batch (linear interpolation; π read at step 5000 here and at step 2000 in the reference; energy measure; one batch size; independently recomputed). So a lock-in (energy measure) also appears under minibatches of 256, but it follows neither a pure step nor a pure epoch clock. A batch series on the same 8 seeds, with predictions fixed before the runs (EXP-108; exploration; independently recomputed; its mean half-point at batch 256 is 127 steps, averaged per seed, against ≈ 133 read off the mean π above), placed the half-point at about 62 steps for batch 1024 (predicted 60–67 by a square-root clock anchored at full batch or at batch 256) and 290 for batch 64 (predicted 240–266; later than predicted): the number of steps to lock-in grew roughly as (N/B)^α with α = 0.54 [0.52, 0.56] (0.556 [0.513, 0.595] without the full-batch point), i.e. with the square root of the steps per epoch. One simple reading, derived in this project (AI-assisted, Appendix I) from the sign-like updates of Adam (Li et al., 2024, whose sign model gives an expected update ∝ √B·μ/σ while √B·μ/σ is small; cf. the Adam SDE of Malladi et al., 2022), is that lock-in occurs once the data-driven drift has covered a fixed distance; it predicts t½ ∝ 1/(lr·√B). At double learning rate (EXP-109, 2 of the seeds, pilot, not independently recomputed) the half-point halved at batch 1024 (≈ 31 steps) but shrank only by ≈ ×0.75 at batch 64, so this reading is at best partial. **Confirmation (N54b, frozen before any run, 10 fresh seeds, batch 1024/256/64 at learning rates 10⁻³ and 2·10⁻³, 840 runs; independently recomputed).** At lr 10⁻³ the mean slope of log t½ against log(N/B) was α = 0.583 [0.560, 0.606] (registered square-root band [0.3, 0.7]; Holm-p 5·10⁻⁶), with half-points of 58, 114 and 289 steps. Doubling the learning rate raised the slope in every seed (Δα = +0.139 [0.098, 0.178], 10/10, Holm-p 6·10⁻⁵): it shortened lock-in to 0.58 [0.54, 0.62] of its duration at batch 1024 but only to 0.85 [0.78, 0.92] at batch 64. So the square-root clock replicates on fresh seeds at the default learning rate, and the simplest sign-drift model (t½ ∝ 1/(lr·√B)) does not suffice, mainly at small batches (Δα between batch 256 and 64: +0.21 [0.13, 0.29]; between 1024 and 256: +0.07 [−0.01, 0.14]); log t½ is curved (two-point slopes 0.48 and 0.68 at lr 10⁻³), so α is an average slope over N/B = 3.7–60, not a clean power law. In a model comparison fixed before the N54b results (exploration), t½ ≈ 23.6·√(N/B)/λ + 2.2·(N/B) (λ = lr/10⁻³; a drift term plus about two epochs) fitted the six cell means better than a free power law. In our search (29 September 2026) we did not find a prior measurement of a square-root batch-size scaling of an early lock-in time at fixed learning rate.

**Confirmation (L0, frozen before any run, 10 fresh seeds; Sitzung 46, independently recomputed).** The steering under i.i.d. minibatches was confirmed at batch 256 (Δ_pow = 9.43 [9.06, 9.81], 10/10 seeds, Holm-p 4·10⁻¹²) and at batch 64 (9.60 [8.79, 10.46], 10/10, Holm-p 3·10⁻⁹). At batch 64, 12 of 20 runs had not settled at the end of the budget (final train accuracy below 0.99; descriptive, post hoc as announced; H2 therefore claims steering at the end of the budget, not in converged networks). Data order and the lock-in window were not part of this confirmation (the lock-in window under minibatches is confirmed separately in N54b, above). **Long epochs (EXP-106, pilot, 8 seeds, batch 16, weight decay 0.1).** Steering held (Δ_pow = 9.21 [8.82, 9.60]); by the pre-set rule the conflict was not testable (no shift on the engineered splits, order-share difference [−0.005, 0.012]; order-alone reading 'unclear', peak in O in 3/8 random-split runs). Descriptively, on random splits the order raised the order share by +0.16 [0.07, 0.24] (+0.14 without the three seeds seen in the probe; about 0 in the three seeds whose i.i.d. run already carried 0.20–0.24 on O), and on the engineered splits it delayed generalization by 0.67 log2 [0.42, 0.93] (≈ ×1.6, 15/16 pairs; no delay on random splits). In this pilot the order thus shifted the spectrum on random splits but not on engineered splits, where it only cost time; this is consistent with LeDoux's remark that the importance of order scales inversely with the content signal of the data.


## Anhang K (Kurzfassung, Entwurf Sitzung 48): Towards language models (exploration, not part of the confirmed claims)

*All results in this appendix are explorations on seeds 3800–3809 (and 3850–3851), with readings fixed before each run; they are not confirmations. The 60 000-step result and the r0-long and 10k checks were independently recomputed; the others were not.*

**Question.** Does steering by the training set (Result 1) survive when the task is trained the way a language model would see it? We changed one property at a time in a trainer that is bit-identical to the Section 2 setup when all switches are off (EXP-102; checked on 3 seeds, split F, at snapshots 50, 250, 1000 and 2000), with the engineered splits F and G of 10 seeds and 2000 full-batch steps.

**Format.** An explicit operator token (a + b =), a softmax over the whole vocabulary, and tied input/output embeddings each kept the steering clearly positive (pre-set reading 'holds'; paired differences to the reference between −0.76 and −0.05, CIs including 0): Δ_pow 7.41 [6.74, 8.17], 7.99 [7.52, 8.48] and 7.28 [6.38, 8.21] against 8.04 [7.59, 8.51] for the reference, 10/10 seeds each. With all three switches together and i.i.d. minibatches of 256 (EXP-112, 5000 steps), Δ_pow was 9.96 [9.44, 10.47] against 9.86 [9.43, 10.28] for the reference at the same batch size (paired +0.10 [−0.20, 0.40]).

**Next-token loss.** A language model also predicts the operands. With the loss on every position, the network must predict b after a, which it can only memorize for the training pairs. This delayed grokking by more than an order of magnitude (median 750 steps with the loss on all positions except b, 20/20 runs by 2000; 9 000–46 000 steps with it, 9/10 seeds by 60 000). After grokking Δ_pow was 4.40 [3.83, 4.91], below the reference in 9/9 seeds (paired −3.75 [−4.68, −2.97]), i.e. about 55 % of its value at step 2000; the pre-set reading was 'unclear' because the interval contains half the reference value. The comparison is at different step counts (22 000–47 000 against 2000). Training the reference longer did not close the gap in this exploration: its Δ_pow rose from 8.04 at step 2000 to 10.44 at 10 000 and 10.54 at 30 000, and at equal step counts the loss on all positions stayed below it in 10/10 seeds (−7.36 [−8.22, −6.31] at 10 000, −6.26 [−7.20, −5.15] at 30 000; exploration, independently recomputed). Equal step counts are not equal stages, however: with the loss on all positions only 1/20 runs had generalized by 10 000 and 13/20 by 30 000; in the 4 seeds in which both runs had generalized by 30 000 the gap was still negative in each (−2.6 to −6.8). Without the loss on the b position the steering held in full batch (8.26 [7.55, 8.96]), but under minibatches of 256 in the full language-model format it was smaller and grokking slower at 5000 steps (8.24 [6.78, 9.72]; paired −1.62 [−3.16, −0.13]; 13/20 runs generalized). At 10 000 steps 16/20 runs had generalized and the paired gap was −1.15 [−2.40, 0.08] (8/10 seeds negative), which by the pre-set rule reads as 'only delayed' (independently recomputed): no lasting weakening was shown, which is not the same as equality (the count lies exactly at the rule's threshold of 16/20, and the upper CI bound only just exceeds 0: 0.08, 0.06–0.14 across bootstrap seeds). Informally, a similar pattern (full-sequence loss favouring a memorized table over the algorithm) was reported for a composition task in the dihedral group D3 in a blog post (read via a summary tool only); we did not find a measurement of this delay or of its effect on data steering.

**Token frequencies.** In a language model, token counts could shape the number embeddings without any arithmetic. In the Section 2 model (answer loss, full batch), splits with exactly equal counts of every a, b and c = a + b (every number 34 times in every role; EXP-104, 2 seeds) still steered (Δ_pow > 1 in both seeds at all three levels of symmetry breaking, 6.0–10.1; not comparable in size with Result 1, because their mask target share is 0.65–0.74 against 0.17–0.19), so there the steering does not need a frequency signal. Nearly shift-invariant versions of these splits generalized in only 2 of 8 runs by 8000 steps, against 4/4 after stronger symmetry breaking, in line with the observation of Tomàs Bernal et al. (2026) for kernels that symmetric training sets can prevent generalization (2 seeds, descriptive; at the two lower levels 6 of 8 runs never generalized, so their Δ_pow describes non-generalized networks).

**A small language model (explorations; Sitzung 50).** A 13.9 M-parameter transformer trained from scratch on TinyStories with interleaved arithmetic sentences (tied embeddings; loss on the text and on the answer tokens) learned the facts only when they were frequent enough: with 8.7 % arithmetic tokens (EXP-105, 2 seeds, 10 000 steps) one seed began to learn them and the other did not; with about 79 % arithmetic tokens (EXP-114, 1 seed, P1-type splits) both runs of the F/G pair learned them between steps 1750 and 2000 and reached 0.98 and 0.99 accuracy on held-out facts by step 4000, and Δ_pow on the number-token rows was 5.60. In both the language model and the Section 2 model, the steering became visible together with the fit to the training pairs: in the Section 2 model (EXP-103, 50 seeds, post hoc) the first snapshot with Δ_pow > 1 and the first with training accuracy ≥ 0.1 in both arms had the same median (step 75); in the language model this onset came only after thousands of steps. With tied embeddings the number rows also serve as output vectors, and in P1-type splits the counts of each number carry target structure, so a positive Δ_pow in a language model need not come from the pair structure alone; a control with exactly equal counts (EXP-104 splits) is queued. A pre-registered block on 10 fresh seeds (LM1: mean Δ_pow above the minimum effect 1 at step 6000, gated on held-out fact accuracy) is running.

**GPU nondeterminism.** The worst late divergence at batch 64 (seed 3728, split F) is not a code difference: two further runs with `train.py` were bit-identical to each other and to the frozen run at all 13 saved spectra up to step 30 000, while the first repro run deviated from step 15 000; the other two divergences were not rerun and are attributed to the same cause. Long minibatch runs on this GPU are therefore not guaranteed to repeat bit for bit; here this affected 3 of 40 runs, all at batch 64 and each first visible at or after the 15 000-step spectrum (after step 12 500 at the earliest); only one of the three (3728 F) was among the 12 of 20 batch-64 runs that had not settled by the end of the budget (Section 6). **§2 Switch protocol, letzte zwei Sätze:** In the isotropic-Jacobian model at initialization, B's expected first-order gradient at A's targets points partly against A's (about −0.38 of A's own push). **§3 R4, Post-hoc-Exploration:** *Post hoc (exploration on the same units, independently recomputed):* a network's strongest (highest-energy) frequencies rank high in its own split's coherent mask spectrum M0c: its five strongest frequencies sit on average at the 78th percentile of the own split's M0c against about the 50th for the other split of the unit (difference 0.28 [0.26, 0.30] at step 2000, 60/60 units; 0.25 at step 8000; 0.29 already at step 250) and absent at initialization.
