## Abstract (Entwurf, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

Networks that grok modular arithmetic use a sparse set of Fourier features, and which frequencies a network uses is usually treated as an outcome of its initialization. We show that the composition of the training set steers this choice. In pre-registered replications on fresh seeds, engineered training splits enrich five prescribed frequencies in the embedding of a one-layer transformer for addition mod 113 and 97, multiplication mod 113 and the dihedral group D59 (50 seeds per task), and equal-norm embedding perturbations along the steered frequencies reduce the logit margin more than along controls (20 seeds per task; D59 after grokking). In addition mod 113, even an ordinary uniform 30 % split biases the learned frequencies, though far from determining them, and engineered splits generalize in about 6.5× fewer steps than uniform random splits and 7.5× fewer than a control with the same number of pairs per output (geometric means, times bounded at 8000 steps; all 20 units in the same direction). The choice starts to persist within 25 to 50 steps (addition mod 113, learning rate 10⁻³): the sign of an ablation contrast between old and new target frequencies reverses between a data switch after 25 and after 50 steps (it tracks embedding energy), and a switch after 25 steps delays grokking by a factor of about 1.8, whereas a switch to new pairs with the same target frequencies costs no time on average. All of these results use full-batch training. ## 1 Introduction (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43, Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung; Quelle: Langfassung §1, §1b und [[paper/related_work]])

Small networks trained on modular arithmetic first memorize their training pairs and only much later generalize ("grokking"; Power et al. 2022). The generalizing solution computes with a handful of Fourier frequencies of the group (Nanda et al. 2023), or with a few irreducible representations for other finite groups (Chughtai et al. 2023). *Which* frequencies a particular network uses has been described as arbitrary across runs (Chughtai et al. 2023); many frequency sets implement the task equally well (Gromov 2023), and at a fixed training set, hyperparameters and initialization can even change the type of algorithm (Zhong et al. 2023). Recent theory attributes the competition between frequencies to the initialization (He et al. 2026a). For uniform data it derives the order of learned representations from the Fourier statistics of the input encoding (Marchetti et al. 2026). With one-hot tokens that encoding favours no frequency in cyclic groups and ties irreducible representations of equal dimension, and the role of the train–test split has been named as open (He et al. 2026b).

We ask whether the training set itself can decide this choice. In the one-layer transformer of a previous grokking study, with fixed one-hot tokens and full-batch AdamW, we construct training splits whose pair mask concentrates its spectrum on five prescribed frequencies. This lever differs from three known ones. LeDoux (2026) showed that a fixed, structured order of examples sets the fundamental frequency under minibatch training, with the same frequency across random training subsets. Marchetti et al. (2026) work through encoding statistics. In recursive feature machines, training sets that are invariant under a subgroup of the task's symmetry group (for example, with the fixed points of a reflection removed) prevent generalization, and the learned feature matrix then encodes only that subgroup; breaking the symmetry of the training set restores generalization (Tomàs Bernal et al. 2026; kernels only, symmetric splits, no prescribed frequencies). Here only *which* pairs are shown changes, the split has no built-in symmetry, and the targets are prescribed frequencies. In language models and related architectures, data, architecture, optimizer and tokenizer decide whether periodic number features become geometrically separable (Fu et al. 2026). For word embeddings, co-occurrence symmetry explains circular and other low-dimensional structures in theory, matched empirically in language models (Karkada et al. 2026). Our setting isolates the data lever in a task where the learned algorithm is known.

We report three pre-registered results with fresh seeds and frozen analysis manifests. (1) Engineered splits steer the learned frequencies in four tasks, including a non-abelian group, and networks are more sensitive along the steered frequencies; an ordinary random split also biases the choice, though far from determining it (Section 3; sensitivity follows spectral energy). (2) Engineered splits generalize several times faster than uniform random splits and than a control with the same number of pairs per output (Section 4). (3) The choice starts to persist within 25 to 50 full-batch steps (at learning rate 10⁻³). A switch after 25 steps delays grokking by about ×1.8, one after 50 steps does not (it even reaches the threshold earlier than training on the destination split alone), and a switch to new pairs with the same target frequencies costs no time on average (Section 5). That early training can resist later data is familiar from critical learning periods (Achille et al. 2019), the primacy bias (Nikishin et al. 2022) and warm starting (Ash and Adams 2020). In fine-tuning, fixed-clock optimizer memory makes data-order effects first order in the learning rate (Sweeney 2026). What we add is a measured time scale of tens of steps for a prescribed feature choice under a controlled data switch.

Because all of these results use full-batch training, while LeDoux's order effect arises under minibatch training, Section 6 (L0) tests whether the steering survives minibatch training and what happens when a structured example order favours a different frequency than the set. All claims hold for one model family and for constructed splits. The random-split, speed-up and persistence results (R4, R5, Section 5) hold for addition mod 113 only. Explorations, resets, the optimizer clock of the lock-in, and a first-order account are in the appendix. Novelty statements mean "not found in our search", not "new".

## 2 Setup (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43, Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung; Quelle: Langfassung §2)

**Model and training.** One-layer transformer of the original GROKVERSE study (following Nanda et al. 2023, without biases): embedding W_E and positional embedding (d_model = 128), one attention layer with four heads of width 32, a ReLU MLP of width 512, no layer normalization, untied readout. Inputs are (a, b, =); the loss is the cross-entropy at the last position. Training is full-batch AdamW (learning rate 10⁻³, weight decay 1, β = (0.9, 0.98)) on 30 % of all pairs. The two arms of a seed share the initialization; only the training set differs. Confirmation blocks ran on one GPU after a statistical equivalence check against the CPU path; runs compared within a block used the same device.

**Tasks.** Addition mod 113 (primary) and 97, multiplication mod 113 (frequencies in the discrete-logarithm coordinate, Z_112; pairs with a zero operand are not in the group and keep uniform weight in the split construction), and composition in the dihedral group D59 (order 118; the 29 two-dimensional irreducible representations).

**Engineered splits.** Per seed, two disjoint target sets of five frequencies each. Arm F draws 30 % of all pairs without replacement with weights q(a, b) = max(0.05, 1 + ε·w(a, b)), w(a, b) = (1/5) Σ_{k∈T_F} cos(2πk·u(a)/n + φ_k(c)) with the group coordinate u(a), the output c and a random phase per target and output (for D59 an irrep trace); arm G likewise; ε = 2. Token counts are no more unequal than in a random split (coefficient of variation 0.14 in the pilot construction), although their spectrum is also target-enriched (Appendix). The main difference lies in the spectrum of the pair mask (centred target share 0.194 against 0.089 for a random split).

**Switch protocol (Result 3; addition mod 113).** A common held-out test set (20 %) is removed first; two pair-disjoint training sets A and B of 3831 pairs pick their pairs in alternation from their own weighted orders (steering strength calibrated to a mask target share of 0.185). A run trains t steps on A, switches to B (optimizer state kept) and trains a fixed further budget; each seed runs both directions. In the isotropic-Jacobian model at initialization, B's expected first-order gradient at A's targets points partly against A's (about −0.38 of A's own push). This is an expectation, not a measured gradient, and how the term acts after 25–50 steps was not measured.

**Measures.** On the group-element rows X of W_E, P_k(X) is the power in the Fourier (irrep) subspace of k. *Steering*: Δ_pow = log[P_F(T_F)/P_F(T_G)] + log[P_G(T_G)/P_G(T_F)] at step 2000. *Margin sensitivity*: the drop of the mean correct-class logit margin on pairs outside both splits after a step X − δ·u_S, δ = 0.02‖X‖, along the arm's own target set S (u_S = P_S X/‖P_S X‖, the normalized projection of X onto the frequency (irrep) subspaces of S), minus the mean drop along four control sets (drawn without both target sets and the harmonics 2k), averaged over both arms. *Persistence*: π = (L_switch − L_pureB)/(L_pureA − L_pureB), L = log[P(T_A)/P(T_B)] of the final embedding, averaged over both switch directions (an unbounded affine index, not a probability). *Ablation contrast*: accuracy after removing the new target subspaces from W_E minus accuracy after removing the old ones. *Time to generalization*: first evaluation with test accuracy ≥ 0.95, evaluated every 25 steps and bounded at a fixed horizon: in R5 a stable event (≥ 0.95 at s, s + 25 and s + 50 after train accuracy ≥ 0.99), bounded at 8000 steps; in R6/R8 the first crossing counted from step 0, bounded at 2000, compared as log2 ratios with the destination-only run.

**Protocol.** Every confirmation block was registered before its runs: hypotheses, primary measure, minimum effect, family with Holm correction, sample size, a reserved block of fresh seeds, and the previously seen data with the decisions they influenced. A committed manifest stores code hashes; a wrapper checks them before, during and after training and binds every output to the manifest, and the analysis accepts only bound runs. In most blocks one frozen job was first run as a smoke test and counted as a regular run. Intervals are percentile bootstrap intervals over seeds. Pilots are reported separately as explorations. Blocks are named by short identifiers (P1, R4, …); Table 1 maps each identifier to its hypothesis, manifest commit, fresh seeds and number of runs (generated from the frozen manifests, `figures/table_registrations.py`).

## 3 Result 1: the composition of the training set steers the learned frequencies (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**Steering (P1, pre-registered replication on 50 fresh seeds per task; tasks chosen as steerable in pilots).** For each seed we built two engineered splits F and G of the same size whose pair masks concentrate their spectrum on five disjoint target frequencies (irreducible representations for D59), and trained the same initialization on each. Only the split differs, so the comparison is a paired intervention. At step 2000 the relative W_E energy of the own against the other targets, Δ_pow, was 8.24 [7.92, 8.54] for addition mod 113, 8.09 [7.75, 8.43] for multiplication mod 113, 6.11 [5.79, 6.43] for addition mod 97 and 2.64 [2.55, 2.73] for D59, positive in 50/50 seeds in every task (Holm-p 1.5·10⁻⁵, worst case). Descriptively, the five own targets carried on average 0.80 of the embedding's group power in addition mod 113 (chance 0.089), 0.79 in multiplication mod 113, 0.69 in addition mod 97 and 0.44 in D59 (chance 0.17). For D59, step 2000 is before grokking; after grokking (P2f, 12 000 steps) the own irreducible representations carried a median 0.98 of the energy. The steering also held when one hyperparameter deviated (R1; weakest where few runs had generalized by step 2000), in a shared-embedding MLP (at 10 000 steps, after grokking), at 4× width, in a two-layer transformer (smaller: −2.70 [−3.16, −2.18] against one layer; R7), and for non-modular integer addition, where no network generalized, so there it is a property of the representation only (P4). Details in Appendix A.

**Functional sensitivity (P2d, P2e, P2f).** A step of norm 0.02·‖X‖ (X = number rows of W_E) along the steered frequencies reduced the mean correct-class logit margin on pairs outside both splits more than steps along four pre-specified control sets: 0.250 [0.229, 0.270] (addition mod 113, 4000 steps), 0.291 [0.268, 0.308] (multiplication mod 113, 8000 steps), 0.294 [0.275, 0.312] (addition mod 97, 8000 steps), each 20/20 seeds, and D = 0.233 [0.214, 0.253] for D59 after grokking (12 000 steps, 20/20). In the addition-mod-113 switch models where we tested it (not the P2d–P2f models), this sensitivity followed the energy distribution: at equal removed energy the dominant set had no advantage. We therefore claim sensitivity, not use beyond energy. Three earlier registered functional variants were not evaluable, aborted or not confirmed; they are reported in Appendix B.

**An ordinary random split also shapes the choice (R4, addition mod 113, 60 fresh units).** Each unit crossed two initializations with two uniform 30 % splits (8000 steps). Networks sharing a split had more similar embedding spectra than networks sharing nothing: after 8000 steps, Δ_split = 0.118 [0.079, 0.157] in an energy-weighted cosine (48/60 units positive), and before grokking the rank order of all 56 frequencies was also more alike (Spearman at step 2000, 0.33 [0.30, 0.36]). Descriptively the shared split raised similarity more than a shared initialization, but it is far from determining the choice: networks with the same split share on average about one of their five strongest frequencies, against about one half without a shared split (chance 25/56 ≈ 0.45). This concerns representation energy, not use (no ablation), in one task and one architecture. *Post hoc (exploration on the same units, independently recomputed):* the frequencies a network ends up using rank high in its own split's coherent mask spectrum M0c: its five strongest frequencies sit on average at the 78th percentile of the own split's M0c against about the 50th for the other split of the unit (difference 0.28 [0.26, 0.30] at step 2000, 60/60 units; 0.25 at step 8000), already from step 250 on and absent at initialization. Because the splits are assigned at random and independently of the initialization, this difference is an effect of the split; that it acts through the mask spectrum is consistent with the first-order account (Appendix G) but not isolated here. The engineered splits of Result 1 thus amplify a mechanism that already acts on ordinary random splits.

**What this shows and what it does not.** In a paired design with engineered splits, the choice of training pairs enriches the prescribed frequencies in four tasks, with fixed one-hot tokens and full-batch training. Section 6 (L0) tests whether the steering survives minibatch training and a competing order signal. LeDoux (2026) reports the same frequency across random training subsets under a structured example order at p = 9973. Plausibly that is a different regime (not measured), dominated by the order signal and with ≈ 80× more pairs. The spectral fluctuations of a uniform random subset shrink roughly as 1/√n, so there they are about 9× smaller.

## 4 Result 2: engineered splits generalize earlier (R5; Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**Design (pre-registered, 20 fresh units, addition mod 113, 8000 steps).** Per unit, seven training sets of 3831 pairs share one held-out test set and one initialization: a uniform random split R, engineered splits with ε = 2 for two target sets and ε = 1 and 8 for one of them, and for each ε = 2 split a control D with exactly the same number of training pairs per output value (per anti-diagonal), but with positions drawn uniformly within each anti-diagonal. The event is a stable rise of test accuracy to ≥ 0.95 after train accuracy ≥ 0.99; times are bounded at 8000 steps.

**Result.** The engineered split generalized in ×6.5 [5.3, 7.9] fewer steps than the random split (target set A) and ×7.5 [6.5, 8.6] fewer than the matched control (mean over both target sets; geometric means of bounded time ratios; 20/20 units in the same direction for both; Holm-p 2·10⁻⁵). Higher ε was faster (ε = 1 took ×14.4 [12.4, 16.6] as long as ε = 8; secondary, outside the family; ε changes pair selection and truncation together, so this is not by itself evidence for concentration). Median bounded times were 775–813 steps (ε = 2), 6163 (R) and 6850–7513 (D). By step 8000, 16/20 random-split units and 14/20 and 11/20 control units had generalized; the others count at the bound, so the ratios are conservative. All censoring is administrative at the common bound of 8000 steps, so the mean bounded time is the restricted mean survival time; post hoc, its ratios are 5.8 [4.6, 7.6] (R against ε = 2, targets A) and 6.9 [6.0, 8.1] (D against ε = 2), consistent with the registered geometric-mean ratios.

**What this shows and what it does not.** At matched per-output counts, the arrangement of the pairs within the anti-diagonals, which carries the spectral concentration of the mask, accounts for the speed-up relative to D; the output marginals alone do not produce it. D matches only the frozen statistics, so finer geometric differences are not excluded. "Earlier" means an earlier stable rise within 8000 steps, not a higher final accuracy. That early task-aligned embedding structure shortens the grokking delay is known from other interventions (Howe 2026b; GrokTransfer). The contribution here is that the choice of training pairs alone produces it, at matched size, initialization and test set. Post hoc, a higher early target share goes with earlier generalization; whether the speed-up runs entirely through this early signal is open. Limits: one task, one architecture and optimizer setting (lr 10⁻³, weight decay 1), train fraction 30 %, ε = 2 for the primary contrasts.

## 5 Result 3: the choice starts to persist within 25–50 steps (K4, K4c, R6, R8; addition mod 113; Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**Persistence after a switch (P3, N28; pre-registered, 30 fresh seeds each).** After 25 steps on a first engineered split and 2000 steps on a pair-disjoint second split, final models sat at 0.351 of the normalized log-energy distance between the two pure endpoints (persistence π), 0.293 [0.263, 0.322] above any tested static additive trace (30/30), i.e. above the π of the pure-destination embedding plus c times the embedding change of the first 25 steps, maximized over c ∈ [0, 1]. Zeroing Adam's first moment at the switch lowered π from 0.340 to 0.189, and ten extra pre-switch steps with the same reset compensated this (N28; non-inferiority, margin 0.05). This is compatible with an "exposure" reading, not a proof of it. That the first moment carries such a signal is known from other settings (Sweeney 2026; Xu 2026). Post hoc, a scalar Adam model puts it at 6.5–10.8 update-equivalents at t = 25–35 (Appendix).

**An ablation contrast reverses (N29, 20 fresh seeds).** Removing all five target frequencies from W_E after a switch and 4000 further steps: after a switch at step 50, removing the old targets cost 0.903 [0.846, 0.953] more test accuracy than removing the new ones (20/20 seeds above 0.3); after a switch at step 25, removing the new targets cost 0.603 [0.498, 0.700] more (17/20). The embedding energy points the same way (post hoc), so the contrast does not separate reliance from energy. The two time points bracket a reversal; a network-wide or irreversible commitment is not shown. 19/20 seeds generalized; restricted to them, the contrasts are 0.898 and 0.591.

**A switch inside this window costs time (R6, 30 fresh seeds).** With b the first step with test accuracy ≥ 0.95 (bounded at 2000) and C(t) the log2 time ratio against training on the destination split only, a switch at step 25 delayed grokking by C(25) = +0.85 [0.73, 0.96] (a factor ≈ 1.8; 29/30 seeds) and more than a switch at step 50 (C(25) − C(50) = +1.25 [0.97, 1.50]; 28/30). Across the grid (t = 10, 20, 25, 30, 40, 50, 75), the mean cost was positive at t = 10–40 (at 40 only +0.27, 22/30) and negative at 50 and 75. C(t) compares regimes: t destination updates are replaced by source updates, no mixed A∪B control was run, and time to threshold is evaluated every 25 steps. All 60 destination-only runs generalized by step 2000; of the switch runs, 20/30 and 22/30 did at t = 25 (both directions) and 17/30 and 16/30 at t = 20, and at least 28/30 at t ≥ 40. The others count at the bound, so C(t) underestimates the delay where it is largest. The confirmed difference between 25 and 50 argues against an exposure-only reading, which predicts C(50) ≥ C(25).

**Only a change of targets costs (R8, 25 fresh seeds).** A switch at step 25 to a pair-disjoint split with the same five targets and matched steering strength did not delay grokking on average (−0.15 log2; 8/25 seeds slower), whereas the switch to other targets did (+0.66; 24/25); difference +0.81 [0.57, 1.04]. The cost of the other-target switch here (+0.66 [0.48, 0.83]) and C(25) in R6 (+0.85 [0.73, 0.96]) are separate estimates on different fresh seeds and splits; their intervals overlap. The delay is therefore not a generic cost of an early change of training pairs, nor of fewer destination updates alone. Because the same-target split has partly reversed wave phases, a pure phase conflict is not excluded. Several switch runs had not generalized by step 2000 (A→B 20/25, B→A 15/25 did), so C_AB is bounded. One task, one architecture.

## 6 L0: set composition against data order under minibatch training (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43, Machbarkeitsbegründung Sitzung 44; nur Methode, Ergebnis ausstehend)

**Question.** All results above use full-batch training. LeDoux (2026) found, under minibatch training with a fixed, structured example order, the same fundamental frequency across random training subsets. We ask whether the steering by the set survives minibatch training, and what happens when an order favours a different frequency than the set.

**Design (pilot EXP-100; the confirmation follows as its own registration).** Addition mod 113, the setup of Section 2. Per seed, engineered splits F and G whose five targets exclude the frequencies favoured by the order, and a uniform random split R. Arms: full batch for 2000 and for 30 000 steps (a budget-matched reference); i.i.d. minibatches of 256 and of 64 pairs (AdamW); SGD with momentum 0.9 (batch 256; learning rate calibrated beforehand on separate runs); and a fixed stride order after LeDoux, pairs sorted by (a mod ⌊√p⌋, a) = (a mod 10, a), identical in every epoch (batch 256). The stride order puts its spectral weight on frequency 11 (48 %), then 23, 34, 12, 45 and 22 (O); the sets carry no more weight on O than chance. The random split with i.i.d. and with stride order measures the order effect alone. A feasibility step before the pilot tests whether the stride order acts at all at 30 % data, at batch 256 and at batch 64; if it acts only at batch 64, the conflict arm is also run there, and if it acts at neither, the conflict is reported as not testable at 30 % data, as fixed before the runs. Rationale, computed before any run from the split files (first order: no optimizer, no weight decay; at most a necessary condition): under full-batch training the frequency choice starts to persist within the first 25–50 steps (Result 3), and summed over whole epochs the stride batches reproduce the set. On the random split (mean over 8 seeds), the excess order-frequency share of the summed batch masks over i.i.d. batches is 0.005 after 50 steps at batch 256 (15 steps per epoch); at batch 64 (60 steps per epoch) it is 0.147 after 25 steps, 0.047 after 50 steps and 0 after one full epoch. The value at 50 steps lies at the edge of our criterion, half of the engineered split's target excess (0.5 × 0.094 = 0.047; 2 of 8 seeds fall below it), so whether the order can compete within the lock-in window at batch 64 is open and is decided empirically. LeDoux's order effect was strong at about 1170 steps per epoch (only the stride order generalized) and modest at about 10 steps per epoch (p = 97, 26.5 % data; fixed orders ≈ 20 % faster); our regime at batch 256 lies on the modest side.

**Readings (fixed before the runs).** Steering under minibatch: Δ_pow (as in Section 3) with confidence intervals per arm, also at equal step count (2000). Conflict: "set wins", "order wins" or "both act", judged only if the order is shown to act (otherwise "not testable"); measured by Δ_pow under the stride order, the order frequencies' share of the embedding energy, and the peak frequency. Speed: time to generalization in steps and epochs, engineered against random split.

## 7 Discussion and limitations (Kurzfassung, Sitzung 43; Fassung 2 nach unabhängiger Prüfung)

**What the three results say together.** In small networks trained full-batch on these tasks, which pairs are shown steers which frequencies the embedding favours, strongly for engineered splits and weakly for random ones (Result 1; measured as embedding energy). In addition mod 113 and one architecture, engineered splits also generalize several times faster at fixed size (Result 2). In the same setting, and at the default learning rate and weight decay, the choice starts to persist within 25 to 50 steps, as measured by embedding energy and by an ablation contrast that does not separate reliance from energy (Result 3). None of this requires changing the tokens, the architecture or the loss.

**Limitations.** (i) *Regime:* unless stated otherwise, results use full-batch AdamW (lr 10⁻³, weight decay 1, β = 0.9/0.98) and a train fraction of 30 %. R1 varies one hyperparameter at a time for the steering. The reversal of Result 3 held at double learning rate but its switching side was not confirmed at 0.3× weight decay (Appendix, R2). Minibatch training and data order are tested only in L0 (Section 6). (ii) *Scope:* steering holds in four tasks; other architectures (MLP, 4× width, two layers) and functional sensitivity were each tested in fewer settings (Appendix). R4, R5 and Result 3 hold for addition mod 113 in one architecture only. (iii) *Measures:* functional sensitivity follows spectral energy in the addition-mod-113 switch models where this was tested (not in the P2d–P2f models). We make no claim about use beyond energy. Resets (Appendix) localize sensitivity, not mechanism. (iv) *Time scales* are counted in optimizer steps. The persistence does not follow the step count alone (a descriptive reading of R3; its Λ clock was not confirmed). With Adam's moment windows rescaled to summed learning rate, it followed summed learning rate in two confirmed cells (R9, addition mod 113, π measure; Appendix). (v) *Horizon:* switch experiments are read at most 2000–4000 steps after the switch (R6/R8: until step 2000 in total). Other final models are read at 2000–12 000 steps. Much longer training is untested. (vi) *Design history:* many decisions were informed by pilots on seeds that were already used, and each registration lists them. Every confirmation used fresh reserved seeds and a frozen analysis manifest or code gate. Post hoc statements (e.g. energy direction, scalar Adam estimate, equal-energy test) are not confirmations.
